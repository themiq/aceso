    "Why do I feel awful?" — the basics check

    Sometimes feeling terrible is partly your body asking for something simple. Go through these one at a time. It sounds silly. It works surprisingly often. (Inspired by the brilliant You Feel Like Shit guide.)

    🧊 The cold-water trickSplash cold water on your face, or hold something cold on your wrists or the back of your neck, for 30 seconds. It triggers the "dive reflex" and can slow your heart rate fast — great for panic or overwhelming emotion.

    Start here: what mental health actually is

    Everyone has mental health, the same way everyone has physical health. Some days it's great, some days you've got a cold, and some seasons you need real care. None of it is a character flaw.

    the short version
    • Struggling is human, common and treatable — about 1 in 5 people deal with a mental health condition in any given year.
    • Your brain is an organ. It responds to sleep, food, stress, people, hormones and history.
    • Feeling better is usually a bunch of small things, not one big fix.

    The stress bucket 🪣

    Picture a bucket. Everything stressful pours in: work, money, health, relationships, bad sleep, the news, old hurts. Everyone's bucket is a different size — some of us were handed smaller ones by genetics or tough childhoods. When the bucket overflows, that's when people struggle.

    The good news: buckets have taps. Coping things — rest, people, movement, therapy, fun, saying no — let some water out. Play with it:

    Your window of tolerance 🪟

    Psychologists talk about a "window" where you can handle what life throws at you. Above the window you're hyper-aroused: anxious, panicky, angry, can't sit still. Below it you're hypo-aroused: numb, flat, shut down, foggy. Stress and trauma can make the window narrower. Lots of the tools in this guide are about two things: noticing when you've left your window, and gently getting back in.

    ⬆️ Too wired?Slow down: long out-breaths, cold water, grounding, a slow walk, low lights, a weighted blanket.
    ⬇️ Too flat?Wake up gently: movement, strong tastes (mints, sour sweets), music, cold air, talking to someone.

    Feelings are messengers, not monsters

    Anxiety says "something might be dangerous". Sadness says "something mattered and it's gone". Anger says "a line got crossed". Loneliness says "you need your people". The feelings aren't the problem — it's when they get stuck on full volume, or show up when there's no real threat, that life gets hard. You don't have to get rid of feelings; you learn to listen to them and turn the volume down.

    Naming feelings actually helps (here's why)

    Research on "affect labelling" shows that simply putting a feeling into words — "I'm feeling anxious and a bit ashamed" — can reduce how intense it feels. The more specific the better: "irritated" or "disappointed" beats "bad". Apps like How We Feel give you a big menu of feeling-words to choose from.

    chapter two

    Anxiety, worry & panic

    Anxiety is your body's smoke alarm. It's meant to keep you safe — but sometimes it goes off for burnt toast, or keeps ringing long after the danger's passed.

    the short version
    • Anxiety is uncomfortable but not dangerous. A panic attack cannot hurt you, and it always passes (usually within 10–20 minutes).
    • Avoiding scary things brings quick relief but makes anxiety bigger over time.
    • Slow breathing, grounding and gently facing fears are the best-proven tools. CBT works really well for anxiety.

    What it can feel like

    • Racing heart, tight chest, shaky or short breath
    • Stomach churning, needing the loo
    • "What if…" thoughts on repeat
    • Feeling on edge, jumpy, restless
    • Trouble sleeping or concentrating
    • Feeling unreal or detached (it's called derealisation, and it's harmless)

    Why it happens

    When your brain senses a threat, it releases adrenaline to help you fight or run. Your heart pumps faster, breathing speeds up, muscles tense. Brilliant for escaping a bear. Less useful for a work email. Genetics, stress, caffeine, poor sleep, past experiences and big life changes can all turn the alarm's sensitivity up.

    During a panic attack

    The "it's just a wave" script
    1. Name it. "This is a panic attack. It feels awful, but it is not dangerous. It will pass."
    2. Don't fight it. Fighting adds fuel. Imagine floating on top of the wave instead of swimming against it.
    3. Breathe out longer than in. In for 4, out for 6. Or the breathing bubble.
    4. Ground. Feet on floor. Press your palms together. Name 5 things you can see.
    5. Cold. Cold water on your face, or an ice cube in your hand.
    6. Wait. Adrenaline physically can't keep surging forever. It peaks and falls.

    For everyday worry

    ⏰ Worry timePick a 15-minute "worry appointment" each day. When a worry pops up outside it, jot it down and tell it, "Not now — 6pm." At 6pm, look at the list. Many worries will have shrunk. For the rest, ask: can I do something about this? If yes, plan one small step. If no, practise letting it float by.
    🪜 The fear ladderWrite the scary thing at the top of a ladder, and break it into smaller rungs. Scared of phone calls? Rung 1: listen to a voicemail. Rung 2: call a recorded info line. Rung 3: call a shop to ask the opening hours… Climb one rung at a time, staying until the anxiety drops a bit. This is called exposure, and it's one of the most effective treatments there is.
    🎈 "And then what?"Follow a worry to the end. "I'll mess up the presentation." And then what? "People will think I'm bad at my job." And then what? Often, the worst case turns out to be survivable — uncomfortable, but survivable — and that takes the air out of it.
    Caffeine, phones & other sneaky anxiety boosters
    • Caffeine mimics anxiety in your body. Try cutting back gradually (sudden stops can cause headaches).
    • Low blood sugar causes shakiness and dread — eat regularly.
    • Doom-scrolling keeps your alarm primed. See Screens & scrolling.
    • Alcohol calms you for an evening, then causes rebound anxiety ("hangxiety") the next day.
    • Not enough sleep makes the amygdala (your alarm centre) much more reactive.
    Get more help if… anxiety is stopping you from doing things you need or want to do, you're having frequent panic attacks, or you're avoiding more and more. Generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, OCD and health anxiety are all very treatable with therapy (especially CBT) and sometimes medication. See Getting help.

    Low mood & depression

    Depression isn't just sadness. It's often more like heaviness — like moving through wet sand, everything taking ten times more effort, and the colour drained out of things you used to love.

    the short version
    • Depression lies. It tells you things are hopeless, that you're a burden, that it'll always be this way. Those are symptoms, not facts.
    • Waiting until you feel like doing things doesn't work with depression. Action comes first; motivation follows.
    • It's one of the most treatable conditions there is. Most people get better.

    What it can feel like

    • Low, empty or numb most of the day
    • Losing interest in things you used to enjoy
    • Exhaustion, even after sleep
    • Sleeping or eating much more or less
    • Feeling worthless, guilty, or like a burden
    • Foggy brain, slow thinking
    • Thoughts about death or not wanting to be here

    Low mood vs. depression

    Everyone has low days, especially after a loss, a disappointment, or during winter. If you've felt low most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, and it's affecting your life, it's worth talking to a doctor. That's not dramatic — that's exactly what they're there for.

    A free screening like MHA's can help you put it into words.

    Things that genuinely help

    🐢 Behavioural activation (tiny version)Depression shrinks your life, and a shrunken life feeds depression. Break the loop by scheduling small things: one thing that gives you a sense of achievement (wash one mug) and one thing that gives a flicker of pleasure (sit in the sun for five minutes). Write them down. Do them whether you feel like it or not. Notice afterwards — even a 2% lift counts.
    🪫 The "low battery" day planOn the worst days, the goal is not productivity. It's: eat something, drink something, take your meds, get some light, and get through to bedtime. That's a full day's work when you're depressed. Everything else is a bonus.
    🧺 Hygiene shortcutsCan't face a shower? Wet wipes, dry shampoo, brushing teeth, a clean t-shirt. Can't do dishes? Paper plates for a week is fine. Lowering the bar isn't failing — it's strategy. The excellent book How to Keep House While Drowning by KC Davis is all about this.
    ☀️ Light & movementMorning daylight (even cloudy) helps your body clock and mood. A 10-minute walk is a real, evidence-backed mood booster. No gym, no running, no lycra required.
    Seasonal depression (the winter blues, and beyond)

    Some people get depressed in autumn and winter when days get shorter. Light therapy lamps (10,000 lux, used for 20–30 minutes in the morning), getting outside at midday, and keeping a routine can help a lot. If it's severe, talk to a doctor — SAD is very real and treatable.

    After having a baby

    Postnatal (postpartum) depression and anxiety affect up to 1 in 7 new parents — of any gender — and are not your fault or a sign you're a bad parent. Postpartum Support International has a helpline and free support groups.

    Please reach out if… you're having thoughts of suicide, can't take care of basic needs, or it's been weeks. Depression is treatable with therapy, medication, or both, and a GP / family doctor is a good first step. If thoughts of death are there, read When it gets really dark.

    chapter four

    Stress & burnout

    Stress is when you have too much. Burnout is when you have nothing left. If you've been running on adrenaline and caffeine for months and suddenly can't care about anything — that might be burnout.

    the short version
    • Burnout = exhaustion + cynicism/detachment + feeling ineffective. It's a response to chronic stress, not a personal weakness.
    • Recovering needs real rest and real changes to what's draining you — a weekend off won't fix months of overload.
    • Boundaries are a health intervention.

    Signs you might be burning out

    • Dreading things you used to handle easily
    • Getting ill more often, headaches, stomach issues
    • Feeling numb or cynical about work, school or caregiving
    • Procrastinating more, and feeling like nothing you do matters
    • Snapping at people you love
    • Needing more and more to "switch off" — wine, scrolling, sleep

    What helps

    🔋 Complete the stress cycleFrom Emily & Amelia Nagoski's Burnout: dealing with the cause of stress is different from dealing with the stress itself in your body. Your body needs a signal that it's safe now. Things that complete the cycle: physical activity (the best), deep slow breathing, a proper belly laugh, a 20-second hug, a good cry, creative expression, affection.
    ✂️ The cut listWrite down everything on your plate. Mark each: must do, could delegate, could drop, could do badly. Pick one thing to delegate or drop this week. "Good enough" is a valid standard.
    🙅 A few ways to say no
    • "I'd love to, but I don't have capacity right now."
    • "Can I get back to you tomorrow?" (buys time)
    • "I can do X, but not Y."
    • "That doesn't work for me." (full sentence, no explanation needed)
    🌙 Real rest has typesSleep is just one. There's also mental rest (breaks from thinking), sensory rest (quiet, dim, no screens), social rest (time alone, or with easy people), creative rest (nature, art), and emotional rest (being able to be honest). Which one are you missing most?
    Worth knowing: burnout can overlap with depression, and with conditions like ADHD or autism where people burn out from masking. If rest doesn't help after a while, talk to a doctor. Students and carers burn out too — it's not just a "work" thing.

    chapter five

    Sleep

    Sleep and mental health are best friends who also fight a lot: bad sleep makes everything feel worse, and feeling bad makes it hard to sleep. Gently fixing sleep is one of the most powerful things you can do.

    the short version
    • Wake up at the same time every day — even weekends. It's the single most useful sleep habit.
    • If you can't sleep after ~20 minutes, get up and do something boring in dim light. Go back when sleepy.
    • CBT for insomnia (CBT-i) works better long-term than sleeping pills — and there's a free app for it.

    The cozy sleep routine

    ☀️ Morning
    • Same wake time every day
    • Get daylight in your eyes within an hour (outside is best)
    • Caffeine only before midday-ish
    • Move your body at some point
    🌙 Evening
    • Dim the lights an hour before bed
    • Phone out of reach (or at least on night mode)
    • Something soothing: a bath, a book, a podcast, stretches
    • Cool, dark, quiet room
    • "Brain dump" worries onto paper
    🛏️ Bed is for sleepIf you lie awake for ages, your brain learns "bed = awake and frustrated". Keep bed for sleep (and intimacy). Can't sleep? Get up, sit somewhere dim, read something dull, return when your eyelids are heavy. It feels backwards, but it retrains your brain.
    🐑 For a racing mindTry the "cognitive shuffle": pick a random word like BREAD. For each letter, imagine lots of things starting with it — B: bear, banana, boat, balloon… It gives your brain something to do that's too boring to worry about. Or listen to a sleep story, rain sounds (Rainy Mood) or a cat purring (Purrli).
    What about naps, alcohol and sleeping pills?
    • Naps: short ones (under 30 minutes, before mid-afternoon) are fine. Long late ones can wreck the night.
    • Alcohol helps you fall asleep but makes sleep shallow and broken. You wake up more tired.
    • Sleeping pills can help short-term, but talk to a doctor. Don't mix them with alcohol.
    • Melatonin is mostly useful for body-clock issues (jet lag, shift work), not general insomnia. Ask a pharmacist.
    See a doctor if… you snore loudly and wake up gasping (possible sleep apnoea), you've had insomnia for more than 3 months, or you're sleepy all day despite enough sleep.

    chapter six

    Body basics: food, water, movement, light

    Your mind lives in your body. That doesn't mean "just go for a run" fixes depression — but it does mean caring for the body is a quiet, steady way of caring for the mind.

    the short version
    • Eat regularly. Skipped meals → blood sugar dips → anxiety, irritability, low mood.
    • Any movement counts. Walking is wildly underrated.
    • Time outside, especially around trees and water, measurably lowers stress.

    Food, without the diet culture

    You don't need a perfect diet. Gentle aims: eat something every 3–5 hours, include some protein and fibre when you can, and drink water. If cooking feels impossible, "beige food" or snack plates (cheese, crackers, fruit, nuts) are completely valid. Fed is best.

    🍳 Low-energy mealsToast with peanut butter or eggs. Instant noodles with a handful of frozen veg and an egg. Yoghurt with cereal. A microwave rice pouch with a tin of tuna or beans. Wraps with whatever's in the fridge. Keep a few "emergency meals" in the cupboard for bad days.

    Movement, the kind way

    Exercise has a strong evidence base for helping depression and anxiety — in some studies, as much as therapy or medication for mild-to-moderate depression. But the best movement is the one you'll actually do. Walking, dancing in the kitchen, stretching, gardening, swimming, yoga on YouTube, chasing a dog.

    🚶 Start ridiculously smallPut your shoes on and walk to the end of the street. That's it. If you want to keep going, great. If not, you did it. Free guides: DAREBEE, Yoga With Adriene, Couch to 5K.
    🌳 Green & blue timeAround two hours a week in nature is linked with better wellbeing. Parks count. Tree-lined streets count. Sitting by a river counts. Can't get out? Plants, nature sounds and even nature videos help a little.
    If food and your body feel complicated

    If you're restricting, bingeing, purging, over-exercising, or food and body thoughts take up a lot of your day, you deserve support — not judgement. Eating disorders affect people of every size, gender and age. Reach out to Beat (UK), ANAD (US), or your doctor.

    Also worth checking: thyroid problems, low iron, low vitamin D, B12 deficiency, hormonal changes and chronic illness can all cause low mood, anxiety and fatigue. A basic blood test from your doctor can rule these out.

    chapter seven

    Loneliness & connection

    You can be lonely in a crowd, lonely in a relationship, lonely with hundreds of online friends. Loneliness isn't about how many people are around — it's about feeling unseen.

    the short version
    • Loneliness is incredibly common and nothing to be ashamed of. Most people feel it sometimes.
    • Loneliness makes us more wary of others, which can make it harder to connect. Knowing that helps you push through.
    • Small, regular, low-pressure contact builds connection better than big gestures.

    Small steps toward people

    📬 Low-stakes reach-outs"This made me think of you" + a meme. "How did that thing go?" A voice note. A photo of your lunch. You don't need a reason to message someone, and most people are happy to hear from you — research shows we consistently underestimate how much others appreciate it.
    🔁 Repeat exposureFriendships mostly grow from seeing the same people again and again. Join something regular: a class, a volunteering shift, a running club, a board-game night, a choir, a library group, a Discord server about something you love. Show up a few times before judging it.
    ☕ "Parallel" companyIf talking feels like too much, just be near people. Work from a café or library. Go to a quiet museum. Watch a stream with chat. Body-doubling counts as company.
    🐾 Other kinds of connectionPets, plants, nature, volunteering with animals, online communities, writing letters, connecting with your culture or faith community. Humans need belonging, and it comes in many forms.
    When relationships themselves hurt

    If someone in your life controls you, puts you down, isolates you from others, scares you, or hurts you — that's not your fault, and help exists. In the US, thehotline.org (1-800-799-7233). In the UK, Refuge (0808 2000 247). Elsewhere, Find A Helpline. The Quick Exit button at the top of this page leaves instantly.

    chapter eight

    Grief & loss

    Grief is love with nowhere to go. It doesn't come in neat stages, it doesn't have a deadline, and it isn't only about death.

    the short version
    • There's no right way to grieve. Numbness, anger, relief, guilt and even laughter are all normal.
    • Grief doesn't get smaller — your life slowly grows around it.
    • You can grieve people, pets, relationships, health, jobs, homes, futures you imagined.

    The ball in the box

    Imagine grief as a ball in a box, with a pain button on one side. At first, the ball is huge — it hits the button constantly. Over time, the ball doesn't shrink so much as the box gets bigger, and the ball hits the button less often. But sometimes it still does, out of nowhere — a song, a smell, an anniversary — and it hurts just as much. That's normal. That's love.

    🕯️ Things that can help
    • Say their name. Tell stories. Grief often hurts more when no one mentions the person.
    • Expect "grief bursts" — and plan for hard dates like birthdays and holidays.
    • Keep a few basics going: food, sleep, a little movement.
    • Write them a letter. Keep their voice messages. Make a memory box.
    • Find people who get it — a grief group, online or in person.
    🫂 Disenfranchised griefSome losses aren't recognised by others: a miscarriage, a pet, an ex, a friend from online, a person you had a complicated relationship with, someone who's still alive but changed by illness. Your grief is still real, and it still deserves care.
    Get support if… after many months, grief is still so intense you can't function, or you feel you can't go on without them. That's sometimes called prolonged grief, and grief counselling really helps.

    chapter nine

    The inner critic & self-compassion

    Most of us have a voice in our head that we'd never let anyone else talk to us like. It's often trying, clumsily, to protect us. But it's not telling the truth.

    the short version
    • Thoughts aren't facts. Brains make predictable errors — especially when stressed, tired or low.
    • Self-compassion isn't letting yourself off the hook. Research shows it makes people more motivated and resilient, not less.
    • Try talking to yourself like you'd talk to a friend.

    Common thinking traps

    These are called "cognitive distortions" in CBT. Everyone does them. Tap a card to flip it and see a kinder way to think.

    Practising self-compassion

    💛 The self-compassion breakBased on Dr Kristin Neff's work. When you're struggling, put a hand on your heart and say (silently is fine):
    1. "This is really hard right now." (mindfulness)
    2. "Other people feel this too. I'm not alone." (common humanity)
    3. "May I be kind to myself." (kindness)
    📝 The friend testWrite down what your inner critic is saying. Now imagine your best friend said that about themselves. What would you say back? Write that down. Read it to yourself.
    🧾 Thought recordWhen a thought really stings: write the situation, the thought, the feeling (and how strong, 0–100), the evidence for and against, and a more balanced thought. Re-rate the feeling. Free worksheets at Get Self Help.

    chapter ten

    Anger & big feelings

    Some feelings arrive like a tidal wave: rage, shame, panic, despair. In that moment, your thinking brain goes offline. The trick isn't to never feel them — it's to have a plan for riding them out.

    the short version
    • Anger is valid. Hurting yourself or others isn't. You can feel the first without doing the second.
    • Emotions peak and pass, usually within minutes, if we don't keep feeding them.
    • Change your body first (temperature, movement, breath) — then think.

    TIPP: a DBT crisis skill

    From Dialectical Behaviour Therapy. Designed for when emotions are too high to think straight.

    🧊 T — TemperatureCold water on your face for 30 seconds while holding your breath (or ice packs on your eyes and cheeks). It slows your heart rate quickly.
    🏃 I — Intense exerciseBurn off the adrenaline: run up the stairs, star jumps, fast walk — even 5–10 minutes helps.
    🌬️ P — Paced breathingBreathe out longer than in. About 5–6 breaths per minute. Use the bubble.
    💪 P — Paired muscle relaxationTense a muscle group as you breathe in, release as you breathe out. Notice the difference.
    🛑 STOPAnother DBT skill: Stop — freeze, don't act. Take a step back — breathe. Observe — what's happening inside and out? Proceed mindfully — what would wise-you do?
    🔎 What's under the anger?Anger is often a bodyguard for softer feelings: hurt, fear, embarrassment, feeling powerless or unheard. When you've calmed down, ask: what was I really feeling? What did I need?
    Get help if… anger is damaging your relationships, you're breaking things or scaring people, or you feel out of control. Anger is very workable with therapy — it's one of the most common things people go for.

    chapter eleven

    Trauma & your nervous system

    Trauma isn't just about what happened. It's about what happened inside you — and how your body learned to keep protecting you long after the danger was gone.

    the short version
    • Fight, flight, freeze and fawn are automatic survival responses. You didn't "choose" how you reacted.
    • Your body can stay on high alert after trauma. That's protection, not brokenness.
    • Trauma is very treatable. Trauma-focused CBT and EMDR have strong evidence.

    The four survival responses

    👊 FightAnger, defensiveness, the urge to push back or control.
    🏃 FlightAnxiety, restlessness, overworking, avoiding, wanting to escape.
    🧊 FreezeGoing blank, numb, stuck, spacey — "playing dead".
    🙇 FawnPeople-pleasing, appeasing, losing yourself to keep others happy.

    Signs your nervous system is still on guard

    • Flashbacks, nightmares, or intrusive memories
    • Being very jumpy or always scanning for danger
    • Avoiding places, people or topics that remind you
    • Feeling numb, detached, or like the world isn't real
    • Strong reactions that seem "too big" for the moment
    • Trouble trusting, feeling safe, or relaxing
    🌊 Finding safety in your body
    • Orient: slowly look around the room. Let your eyes land on things. Notice exits, colours, light. This tells your brain "I'm here, now, and it's safe."
    • Hum or sing — it gently stimulates the vagus nerve.
    • Weight & pressure: a weighted blanket, a firm hug, pushing against a wall.
    • Safe place: imagine, in detail, a place you feel completely safe — real or imaginary.
    Please go gently. If you've been through trauma, you don't have to dig into it alone. A trauma-informed therapist can help you process things at a pace that feels safe. If you're in danger right now, or someone is hurting you, see Help now. For sexual assault, RAINN (US) or Find A Helpline elsewhere.

    chapter twelve

    When it gets really dark

    If you're thinking about suicide, or hurting yourself, I want you to know: you're not broken, you're not bad, and you're not alone. You're in a huge amount of pain. And pain can change, even when it feels like it never will.

    the short version
    • Suicidal thoughts are a sign of overwhelming pain, not of weakness. Many, many people have them and go on to feel better.
    • The most intense urges usually pass. Getting through the next few hours safely matters most.
    • Telling someone — a friend, a doctor, a crisis line — is one of the bravest and most effective things you can do.
    🕯️ If you're thinking about suicide right now
    1. Put some distance between you and anything you could use to hurt yourself. Give it to someone, lock it away, or leave the room. This genuinely saves lives — the most intense moments pass.
    2. Don't be alone, if you can help it. Go to where other people are, or call someone to stay on the phone with you.
    3. Contact a crisis line — numbers are here. Text if talking is too much.
    4. Avoid alcohol and drugs right now — they make impulses harder to resist.
    5. Make a deal with yourself to wait. Just today. Just tonight. Just the next hour. You don't have to decide anything now.

    "But I don't want to bother anyone"

    Crisis lines exist specifically for this. The people who answer chose to be there. You won't be wasting their time — reaching out early is exactly what they hope people will do. And the people in your life would almost certainly rather know.

    What actually happens when you call a crisis line?

    Someone trained will answer, and they'll listen. They'll probably ask how you're feeling and whether you're safe right now — they ask this directly because it helps. They won't judge you or tell you off. Most calls end with talking things through and maybe making a plan together. In most places, emergency services are only involved if someone is in immediate danger and can't stay safe — and usually even then they'll try to work it out with you first. You can hang up any time. You can stay anonymous on most lines.

    About self-harm

    Self-harm is often a way of coping with feelings that feel unbearable. It makes sense that you'd reach for something that gives relief — and you deserve ways of coping that don't hurt you. No shame, no lectures.

    🌊 Riding out the urge
    • Delay: set a timer for 15 minutes. Often the urge eases. Then try another 15.
    • Intense but safe sensations: hold ice, snap a hair tie loosely, take a cold shower, eat something very sour or spicy.
    • Draw on your skin with a red pen or marker instead.
    • Release: scream into a pillow, rip up paper, hit a cushion, sprint.
    • Soothe: wrap up in a blanket, cuddle a pet, put on a comfort show.
    • Try the free Calm Harm app.
    If you've hurt yourself and the wound is deep, won't stop bleeding, or you've taken anything (even if you feel okay — some substances cause delayed harm), please get medical help now. Emergency staff are there to help you, not to judge you.

    Reasons to stay (from people who stayed)

    People who've survived suicidal crises often say how glad they are that they're still here — sometimes about things they couldn't have imagined at the time. New people they met. Feeling better on medication they hadn't tried yet. A dog. A morning. Your future isn't written yet. Make a safety plan — it's one of the most effective tools there is.

    chapter thirteen

    Screens, scrolling & digital wellbeing

    The internet can be a lifeline — your people, your communities, your favourite cozy videos. It can also be a slot machine in your pocket designed by very clever people to keep you pulling the lever.

    the short version
    • It's not about zero screen time. It's about whether your use leaves you feeling better or worse.
    • Willpower alone loses to apps designed by teams of engineers. Change your environment instead.
    • Notice how you feel after — that's your best guide.
    📵 Friction is your friend
    • Turn on greyscale (it makes your phone dramatically less appealing).
    • Move tempting apps off your home screen, or log out after each use.
    • Turn off all non-human notifications.
    • Charge your phone outside the bedroom. Buy a cheap alarm clock.
    • Try one sec or DetoxDroid to add a pause before opening apps.
    🧹 Curate your feedMute or unfollow anyone who makes you feel worse about yourself — even friends (mute is invisible). Follow people who make you laugh, learn, or feel calm. You're the gardener of your feed.
    📰 News without the doomPick one or two set times to check the news, from one trusted source. Avoid it in the first hour after waking and the last hour before bed. It's okay to care about the world and protect your mind.

    chapter fourteen

    Substances, habits & recovery

    Most people who drink too much, use drugs, gamble or get stuck in compulsive habits are trying to feel better, or feel less. That makes total sense. It just tends to cost more than it gives, over time.

    the short version
    • Addiction is a health issue, not a moral failure.
    • You don't have to hit "rock bottom" to change, and you don't have to quit forever to start — cutting down counts.
    • Slips are part of recovery, not the end of it.
    🤔 Gentle questions to ask yourself
    • Am I using it to cope with feelings, or to get through the day?
    • Have I tried to cut down and found it hard?
    • Is it affecting my health, relationships, money, work?
    • Do I feel guilty or hide how much I use?
    If some of these ring true, that's not a verdict — it's information. You deserve support.
    🌱 Ways to start
    • Track it honestly for a week — no judgement, just data.
    • Notice your triggers: times, places, feelings, people.
    • Plan alternatives for those moments.
    • Try a sober/dry month and see how you feel.
    • Find your people: SMART Recovery, AA/NA, online communities.
    Important safety note: stopping alcohol or benzodiazepines suddenly after heavy, regular use can be dangerous (seizures are possible). Please talk to a doctor about detoxing safely. And if you do use drugs, harm reduction matters: never use alone, test your substances where possible, and know the signs of overdose. TripSit has a live chat.

    chapter fifteen

    Being yourself: identity & belonging

    A lot of mental pain comes not from what's inside us, but from a world that hasn't made room for who we are. If you're LGBTQ+, neurodivergent, disabled, a person of colour, an immigrant — or just different — your struggles might partly be the weight of that.

    the short version
    • There's nothing wrong with who you are. Stress from discrimination and not fitting in is real, and has a name: minority stress.
    • Finding people like you is one of the most protective things there is.
    • You deserve affirming support — and it exists.

    🏳️‍🌈 LGBTQ+

    LGBTQ+ people have higher rates of anxiety and depression — not because of who they are, but because of stigma, rejection and stress. Having even one accepting adult or affirming space significantly lowers risk for young people. If you're questioning, you don't need to have it figured out. If you're not out, that's your call, and your timeline.

    🌈 Places to startThe Trevor Project & TrevorSpace (youth), The Gender Dysphoria Bible, Trans Lifeline, Switchboard (UK), and the trans health resources from FMHY in the library.

    🧠 Neurodivergence (ADHD, autism & more)

    Many adults — especially women and people of colour — find out they're autistic or have ADHD later in life, often after years of feeling "too much", "lazy", or like they were missing the instruction manual everyone else got. Masking (hiding your traits to fit in) is exhausting and linked with burnout and depression.

    🧩 Brain-friendly tips
    • Body doubling: do tasks alongside someone (in person or on video).
    • Externalise: timers, visual lists, alarms — your brain doesn't have to hold everything.
    • Sensory care: noise-cancelling headphones, soft clothes, dim lights, stims are welcome.
    • Unmask safely: find spaces and people you can be fully yourself with.
    • Learn more: How to ADHD, Embrace Autism, ASAN.

    🌍 Culture, race & belonging

    Racism, discrimination and feeling like an outsider take a real toll on mental health. You may also want a therapist who shares or understands your background — directories like Inclusive Therapists and Therapy for Black Girls can help.

    chapter sixteen

    Money, work & life stuff

    Sometimes it's not your brain chemistry. It's the rent. It's the job. It's the exam, the debt, the housing, the carer duties. No amount of breathing exercises fixes an eviction notice — but practical help exists, and getting it is a mental health move.

    the short version
    • Money worries and mental health feed each other. It's not a personal failing.
    • Free, non-judgemental debt and benefits advice is out there. Use it.
    • Opening the scary envelope is usually less bad than imagining it.
    💸 When money is scary
    • Pick a calm time, make a drink, and open the post. Just open it. Sort into piles.
    • List what's coming in and going out. Even roughly.
    • Prioritise: housing, energy, council tax/rates, food come first.
    • Contact creditors early — many have hardship options, especially if you mention mental health.
    • Get free advice: 211 & NFCC (US), Citizens Advice & StepChange (UK).
    • Avoid payday loans and anyone promising quick fixes.
    💼 Work & study
    • In many countries, mental health conditions are covered by disability law — you may be entitled to reasonable adjustments.
    • Check if your work or school has a free counselling service or Employee Assistance Programme (EAP). Many people don't know they have one.
    • A doctor's note for time off is okay. Mental health is health.
    • Students: talk to your tutor or disability service early. Extensions exist for a reason.
    🤲 Caring for someone elseCarers burn out quietly. Look for carers' organisations, respite care, and carer support groups locally — and remember your needs matter too.

    chapter seventeen

    Getting help: therapy, doctors & medication

    Asking for help can feel like the hardest part. Here's a map of what's out there, how to find it, and what to actually say.

    the short version
    • A GP / family doctor is a good first step in most places.
    • Many countries have free or low-cost therapy you can self-refer to.
    • Finding the right therapist can take a couple of tries. The relationship matters more than the type of therapy.

    What to say to a doctor

    🗣️ A script you can borrow

    "I've been struggling with my mental health. For about [how long] I've been feeling [low / anxious / numb / not sleeping…]. It's affecting my [work / sleep / relationships / eating]. I'd like to talk about what support is available."

    Write it down and read it out, or hand them the paper. Doctors see this every day. If they're dismissive, you're allowed to ask for another doctor.

    Types of therapy (a quick menu)

    Finding it, by place

    • 🇬🇧 England: self-refer to NHS Talking Therapies — free, no GP needed. Scotland, Wales & NI: start with your GP.
    • 🇺🇸 US: check your insurance's directory, Psychology Today, Open Path (sliding scale), community mental health centres, FindTreatment.gov, and university training clinics (cheaper sessions with supervised trainees).
    • 🇦🇺 Australia: ask your GP for a Mental Health Treatment Plan (Medicare-subsidised sessions), or contact Medicare Mental Health.
    • 🇨🇦 Canada: check provincial programs, workplace EAPs and community clinics.
    • Anywhere: workplace/school counselling, charities, and online CBT programmes like MoodGYM or This Way Up.

    About medication

    Medication like antidepressants can be genuinely life-changing for some people, and isn't "the easy way out" or a sign you've failed. A few honest things to know:

    • Many take 2–6 weeks to work fully. Side effects often show up first and ease off.
    • The first one you try might not be the right one. That's normal — it's not that "nothing works for you".
    • Never stop suddenly without talking to your doctor. Tapering matters.
    • Tell your doctor about any other meds, supplements (like St John's Wort) or substances.
    • Medication + therapy together often works best.

    chapter eighteen

    Helping someone you care about

    When someone you love is struggling, it's easy to panic about saying the wrong thing. Here's a secret: you don't need the perfect words. Showing up matters far more.

    the short version
    • Listen more than you fix. "That sounds really hard" goes a long way.
    • Asking directly about suicide does not put the idea in someone's head. It can be a relief.
    • Look after yourself too. You can't pour from an empty cup.
    ✅ Things that help
    • "I'm here. No pressure to talk, but I'm here."
    • "That sounds really hard."
    • "Do you want me to listen, or help problem-solve?"
    • "Want to go for a walk / get food?"
    • Practical help: dropping off meals, helping book an appointment, sitting with them.
    • Keep checking in, even if they don't reply much.
    ❌ Things to avoid
    • "Just think positive!" / "Others have it worse."
    • "Have you tried yoga?" (unless they ask)
    • Making it about you, or getting angry they didn't tell you sooner.
    • Promising to keep a secret if their life is at risk.
    • Disappearing because you don't know what to say.
    💬 If you're worried about suicide
    1. Ask directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Use the word. It shows you can handle the answer.
    2. Listen without judgement. Don't argue or panic.
    3. Help them stay safe — help put distance between them and means of harm.
    4. Connect them to support: a crisis line (you can call together), a doctor, or emergency services if they're in immediate danger.
    5. Follow up in the days after.
    Your wellbeing matters too. Supporting someone can be draining. Set boundaries, talk to someone yourself, and remember you're a supporter, not their therapist. Groups like NAMI run free family support programmes. Practise the conversation with Movember Conversations.

    Your toolkit & safety plan

    A safety plan is a personal list of what helps when things get hard. Clinicians use them because they work. Fill it in on a good-ish day, so it's ready when you need it. Everything stays on your device — nothing is sent anywhere.

    Thoughts, feelings, behaviours that tell me I'm starting to struggle.
    Things that calm or distract me, even a little.
    Where I can go and who I can be around, without having to talk about it.
    Names and numbers.
    My doctor, therapist, and local crisis numbers.
    Things I can remove or put distance from when I'm in a bad place.
    People, pets, dreams, small joys, things I'm curious about. Anything at all.

    Tip: take a photo of your printed plan and keep it on your phone. Share it with someone you trust. The free Stay Alive app can hold one too.
