    A guide for when things are hard

    Practical advice for looking after your head: anxiety, depression, sleep, grief, burnout, getting help, and what to do on the bad nights. Read the bits you need.

      Where to start

      Mental health works like physical health: it varies, and sometimes it needs treatment. In the US, a bit over one in five adults has a diagnosable mental health condition in any year1. Struggling is common. It's also usually treatable.

      The stress bucket

      A model I use a lot comes from the stress-vulnerability model of mental illness2. Imagine a bucket. Stress pours in from everywhere: work, money, health, relationships, bad sleep, unresolved stuff from years ago. Bucket size varies from person to person, mostly because of genes and early life. When it overflows, you struggle, and the thing that tips it over is often small enough that it looks like an overreaction. It isn't. The bucket was full.

      The bucket also has taps: rest, people, exercise, therapy, saying no, things you enjoy. Try drawing yours. Write what's pouring in down one side and what drains it down the other. If the first list is long and the second is two half-hearted items, you know where to start.

      Too wired or too flat

      Psychiatrist Dan Siegel described a "window of tolerance"3, the range where you can handle what's in front of you. Above it you're wired: anxious, panicky, irritable, can't sit still. Below it you shut down: numb, foggy, far away. Stress and trauma shrink the window.

      The two states need opposite fixes, and people often reach for the wrong one. Above the window, slow your body down with long out-breaths, cold water on your face, a slow walk or dim light. Below it, wake your body up with movement, loud music, a sour sweet, cold air or a conversation. Lying in a dark room when you're already shut down usually makes it worse.

      Name it

      Putting a feeling into words reduces activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm system4. Be specific: "disappointed and embarrassed" works better than "bad". If you can't find the word, the free How We Feel app has a long list to pick from.

      Check the boring stuff first

      Before you go digging for deep causes, run through HALT, an acronym from recovery groups: are you hungry, angry, lonely or tired? Each one makes everything look worse, and each can be dealt with today. It's also worth keeping a basic log for two weeks: mood out of ten, hours slept, whether you ate properly, saw anyone, went outside. Patterns show up that you'd never spot otherwise.

      Anxiety and panic

      Anxiety is an alarm system. When it goes off, adrenaline gets you ready to run or fight. That's useful if something is chasing you. It's less useful when the alarm goes off over an email, or keeps ringing for weeks for no clear reason.

      The physical symptoms are scary, but the explanations are dull. Your heart races to get blood to your muscles. Fast breathing lowers the carbon dioxide in your blood, which causes tingling, dizziness and the feeling you can't get enough air, even though you're getting too much. Your stomach churns because digestion shuts down. Some people feel unreal or detached from their body. That's called derealisation or depersonalisation, and it's harmless and goes when the anxiety does.

      During a panic attack

      A panic attack can't hurt you. It feels like you're about to faint, have a heart attack or lose your mind, and none of those happen. Fainting needs a drop in blood pressure, and panic raises it. (The one exception is a fear of blood or needles, which can make people faint; if that's you, tense your arm and leg muscles hard to keep your blood pressure up.) Adrenaline peaks and falls, usually within ten or twenty minutes. Fighting it keeps it going. Instead:

      1. Name it: "This is a panic attack. It will pass."
      2. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in, roughly in for four and out for six. The exercise below will pace you.
      3. Feet flat on the floor, palms pressed together. Name five things you can see.
      4. Cold water on your face or something cold in your hand slows your heart rate.
      5. If you're somewhere safe, stay. Leaving teaches your brain the place was dangerous and makes the next attack there more likely.

      Breathing exercise

      Breathe in while the circle grows, out while it shrinks. Aim for at least six rounds. In 4, out 6 suits most people. Box breathing and 4-7-8 are there if you prefer them.

      Press start when you're ready

      Facing fears in steps

      Avoidance feels like relief and makes fear grow, because you never find out you'd have coped. Gradual exposure is the core of the CBT that guidelines recommend for anxiety5, and you can do a version of it on your own:

      1. List the situations you avoid or dread, and rate each from 0 to 100 for how anxious it makes you.
      2. Put them in order, easiest first. If phone calls are the problem, that might go: listen to a voicemail, call a recorded information line, ring a shop to ask their opening hours, call a friend, book a doctor's appointment.
      3. Before each one, write down what you think will happen. Afterwards, write down what really happened. The gap between the two is what retrains your brain.
      4. Start around 30 or 40. Repeat each step several times, close together, before moving up. Most days beats once a week.
      5. Drop your "safety behaviours" as you go: gripping your phone, avoiding eye contact, over-rehearsing, sitting by the door. They stop you learning you'd be fine without them.

      Older advice said to stay in the situation until your anxiety halves. Current research suggests that isn't necessary. What matters is testing the prediction and seeing it not come true, even if you're still anxious when you leave6. It feels bad at first. That part is the treatment.

      Worry

      Worry postponement has evidence going back to the 1980s7. Pick a fifteen-minute slot at the same time each day, not near bedtime. When a worry shows up at any other time, jot down a couple of words and park it. When the slot comes, go through the list. A lot of it will have stopped mattering.

      Sort whatever's left into two piles. Practical worries are about something real you can act on ("the gas bill is due Friday"): write down the next step and when you'll do it. Hypothetical worries are "what ifs" that may never happen ("what if I get ill and can't work"). There's nothing to solve, so problem-solving them just keeps you going round. Notice the thought, label it ("what-if again"), and put your attention back on what you're doing.

      Following a worry to its end also helps. "I'll mess up the presentation." Then what? "People will think I'm bad at my job." Then what? The worst case is usually unpleasant and survivable.

      Social anxiety and health anxiety

      Social anxiety turns your attention inwards. You monitor your face, your voice, whether you're going red, and that self-monitoring is a big part of what keeps it going8. Put your attention on the other person and what they're saying instead. It feels exposed for the first few goes, then conversations get easier.

      With health anxiety, checking is the trap. Googling symptoms, prodding the same spot and asking people for reassurance all bring a few minutes of relief and make the worry stronger. Set rules (no symptom searches, one check a day at most) and expect the urge to spike before it fades.

      Things that make it worse

      Caffeine causes almost the same body sensations as anxiety and stays in your system for around five hours, so cut down gradually if you drink a lot. Low blood sugar brings shakiness and dread. Alcohol calms you for the evening and leaves you more anxious the next day. Short sleep makes the brain's threat system far more reactive. Regular exercise reduces anxiety for most people.

      See a doctor if anxiety is stopping you doing things, you're having regular panic attacks, or you're avoiding more and more. Generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, OCD and health anxiety all respond well to CBT, sometimes with medication. The free workbooks from the Centre for Clinical Interventions are the best self-help material I know. More on the resources page.

      Depression and low mood

      Depression often feels less like sadness and more like weight. Everything takes effort, things you liked go flat, and you're tired however much you sleep. It can come with guilt, feeling like a burden, foggy thinking, changes in appetite and sleep, and thoughts about death.

      It also distorts your thinking. It tells you things will always be like this, nobody would care, you're the problem. Those thoughts feel like clear-eyed insight. They're symptoms, and they ease as the depression does.

      Everyone has low days. The clinical threshold for depression is feeling low or losing interest in things most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, along with other symptoms that affect your life. If that's you, see a doctor. A free screening from Mental Health America can help you describe it.

      Behavioural activation

      With depression, waiting until you feel like doing things doesn't work, because the feeling doesn't arrive. Behavioural activation flips the order: you act first and motivation catches up. It works about as well as full CBT and is easier to learn9. A version you can do at home:

      1. For three or four days, write down roughly what you did each hour and rate your mood out of ten. Don't change anything yet.
      2. List activities in three groups: things you need to do (a bill, the washing up), things you used to enjoy, and things that matter to you (seeing your sister, finishing a course).
      3. Schedule a few into specific slots over the next week. Make them small: "walk to the post box", not "get fit".
      4. Do them whether or not you feel like it. Rate your mood before and after. Depression is bad at predicting how things will feel, and this shows you.
      5. Add a bit more each week.

      Write plans as "if, then" sentences: "If it's 10am, then I take the dog round the block." That format makes people noticeably more likely to follow through10.

      Rumination

      Depression comes with a lot of going over things: why did this happen, why am I like this. It feels like problem-solving but mostly keeps you stuck. Rumination-focused CBT suggests switching from abstract "why" questions to concrete "how" questions11. "Why am I such a failure?" has no answer. "How could I make tomorrow morning slightly easier?" does. When you catch yourself looping, change something physical too: stand up, go to another room, do something with your hands.

      The worst days

      Lower the bar. Eat something, drink water, take your meds, get some daylight, get to bed. If a shower is too much, brush your teeth and change your shirt. If washing up is too much, buy paper plates. KC Davis's How to Keep House While Drowning is good on this.

      Keep your wake-up time roughly fixed even when you're exhausted. Long lie-ins and afternoon naps feel like relief, but they scramble your body clock and tend to deepen depression. And keep some contact with people, even one text a day. Depression pushes you to withdraw, and withdrawal feeds it.

      Movement and light

      A large 2024 review found exercise had a real effect on depression, with walking or jogging, yoga and strength training doing best12. Harder exercise helped more, but anything helped. Ten minutes outside is a fine start.

      If your mood drops every autumn and lifts in spring, it may be seasonal affective disorder. A 10,000 lux light box for twenty to thirty minutes each morning has good evidence13. Check with a doctor first if you have bipolar disorder or an eye condition.

      Depression around pregnancy and birth is common: around one in seven new mothers screened positive in one large US study14, and partners get it too. Postpartum Support International has a helpline and free groups.

      If you're having thoughts of suicide, can't manage basic care, or it's been going on for weeks, get help now. Depression responds well to therapy, medication or both15, and a GP or family doctor is the usual first step. Mention it if you've ever had spells of unusually high energy, little need for sleep or racing thoughts, because that changes which treatment is safe. See also suicidal thoughts.

      Stress and burnout

      Stress is too much to deal with. Burnout is what's left after months of it. The World Health Organization defines it by three features: exhaustion, feeling cynical or detached about your work, and feeling less effective at it16. In practice that looks like getting ill more, snapping at people, dreading things you used to handle, and needing more wine or more scrolling to switch off. The WHO definition is about jobs, but students, parents and carers burn out the same way.

      Burnout and depression overlap, and the difference matters. Burnout is usually tied to one area of life and lifts when you're away from it. If the flatness follows you on holiday and into everything you used to enjoy, treat it as possible depression and see a doctor.

      Stress lives in your body

      Emily and Amelia Nagoski's book Burnout separates the cause of stress (the deadline, the manager) from the stress itself, which builds up in your body17. Even when you can't fix the cause, you can tell your body the threat has passed. Physical activity does that best. Slow breathing, a proper laugh, a long hug, crying and making something with your hands help too. Do one every day, on purpose.

      Recovering in the evenings

      Research on recovery from work points to four things18: switching off mentally, relaxing, doing something absorbing that's unrelated to work (climbing, an instrument, a language), and having control over your own free time. Half-reading work messages while scrolling gets you none of them. A shutdown routine helps. At the end of the day, write down what's unfinished and what you'll do first tomorrow, close everything, and stop.

      Taking things off the pile

      List everything you're responsible for. Mark each one: must do, could hand off, could drop, or could do badly. Pick one to hand off or drop this week. Doing something adequately is a legitimate choice, and when you're burnt out it's often the only one available.

      Have a few lines ready for saying no: "I don't have capacity for that right now." "Can I get back to you tomorrow?" "I can do this part, not that one." At work, make your manager choose: "I can do A or B by Friday. Which matters more?"

      Talking to work

      If work is the main source and it's safe to, talk to your manager or HR. Reduced hours, different duties or clearer priorities can make a big difference, and in many countries you have a legal right to reasonable adjustments if your mental health counts as a disability. A doctor can sign you off. When you go back, a phased return over a few weeks works better than going straight back to full time.

      Burnout also overlaps with ADHD and autism, where years of masking wear people down. If proper rest hasn't helped after a few weeks, see a doctor.

      Sleep

      Bad sleep makes everything harder, and struggling makes it harder to sleep. Fixing sleep is one of the most effective things you can do for your mental health, and it's more fixable than it feels at 3am.

      The basics

      The single most useful change is a fixed wake-up time, weekends included. It anchors your body clock, and sleepiness at night follows. Get daylight in your eyes within an hour of waking; even an overcast sky is far brighter than indoor light. Keep caffeine to the morning, since a 3pm coffee is still half in your system at bedtime. Wind down for an hour with dim light, and keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet.

      Write tomorrow's to-do list before bed. In one study, people who spent five minutes writing a specific to-do list fell asleep faster than people who wrote about what they'd already done19. Once it's on paper, your brain stops rehearsing it.

      Turn the clock away. Working out how many hours you've got left is a reliable way to stay awake.

      CBT for insomnia

      If you've had trouble sleeping for months, sleep hygiene tips usually won't fix it. CBT for insomnia (CBT-i) is the first-line treatment in doctors' guidelines, ahead of sleeping pills20, and it works for most people who stick with it21. There are two main parts.

      Stimulus control: only go to bed when you're sleepy, which isn't the same as tired. If you're not asleep after what feels like twenty minutes, get up, go somewhere dim, do something dull, and go back when you're sleepy. Repeat as needed. Use the bed only for sleep and sex, so your brain stops linking it with lying awake.

      Sleep restriction: for a week or two, limit your time in bed to roughly how long you're really sleeping (never under about five and a half hours). Keep the wake time fixed and push bedtime later. That builds enough sleep pressure that you drop off fast and sleep more solidly. Once you're sleeping through most of the time in bed, move bedtime earlier by fifteen minutes a week. Expect to be tired for the first week. Don't drive when you're very sleepy, and get medical advice first if you have bipolar disorder, epilepsy, or a job where sleepiness is dangerous.

      The free CBT-i Coach app from the US Department of Veterans Affairs walks you through all of this, and anyone can use it.

      Other things worth knowing

      Short naps before mid-afternoon are fine. Long or late ones cost you the night. Alcohol helps you fall asleep, then breaks up the second half of the night. With screens, what you're doing matters more than the light: arguing online keeps you up longer than a calm programme. Melatonin mainly helps with body-clock problems like jet lag and shift work. Sleeping pills are a short-term option to discuss with a doctor, and never with alcohol.

      For a racing mind, try a "cognitive shuffle": pick a word, and for each letter picture as many unrelated things as you can that start with it. It's boring enough to let you drift off. Recurring nightmares, especially after trauma, can be treated with imagery rehearsal, where you rewrite the dream while awake and practise the new version. Ask a therapist about it.

      See a doctor if insomnia has lasted more than three months, if you snore loudly and wake up gasping (possible sleep apnoea), or if you're sleepy all day despite a full night in bed. More guides on the resources page.

      Food, movement and daylight

      A run won't cure depression. But your brain runs on the same body as everything else, and looking after that body has a slow, steady effect on mood.

      Eating

      Regularity matters most. Skipped meals mean low blood sugar, which feels a lot like anxiety, irritability or despair. Eat something every three to five hours, with some protein and fibre when you can manage it; they keep energy steadier than sugar alone.

      Diet quality may matter too. In a small 2017 trial, people with depression who moved to a Mediterranean-style diet (vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, fish, olive oil, nuts) improved more than a comparison group given social support22. One small trial isn't proof, but it's a cheap experiment.

      If cooking is beyond you, a plate of cheese, crackers, fruit and nuts is a meal. So is peanut butter on toast, instant noodles with frozen veg and an egg, or a microwave rice pouch with a tin of beans. Keep a few in the cupboard for bad days.

      If food has got complicated, whether that's restricting, bingeing, purging, over-exercising or constant thoughts about your body, talk to someone. Eating disorders affect people of every size, age and gender, and they're much easier to treat early. Beat (UK) and ANAD (US) offer free support.

      Moving

      Pick something you'll keep doing. Walking, yoga and strength training all did well in the 2024 review above12, and none needs much kit. Start smaller than feels worthwhile: put your shoes on and walk to the end of the road. Tie it to something you already do ("after the school run, I walk the long way home"), do it with someone so you're less likely to bail, and count anything as a win for the first month. DAREBEE, Yoga With Adriene and the NHS Couch to 5K app are free.

      Getting outside

      A study of about 20,000 people in England found that those who spent at least two hours a week in nature reported better health and wellbeing than those who spent none, whether they did it in one go or several short trips23. Parks, canal paths and tree-lined streets all count. Daylight also keeps your body clock steady, which helps sleep and mood.

      Thyroid problems, low iron, low vitamin D, low B12, hormonal changes and many chronic illnesses can cause low mood, anxiety or exhaustion. Ask your doctor for a basic blood test, especially if the low mood came out of nowhere.

      Loneliness

      You can be lonely in a crowd, in a relationship, or with hundreds of contacts in your phone. It's less about how many people are around and more about whether any of them know you. It's also a health issue: a large review linked strong social relationships with a substantially lower risk of death, comparable in size to well-known risks like smoking24.

      Loneliness makes people more guarded and quicker to read rejection into neutral situations, which makes connecting harder exactly when you need it. The evidence says your pessimism is probably off. People underestimate how much others appreciate hearing from them out of the blue25, and after talking to someone new, people consistently think the other person liked them less than they really did26.

      Making friends as an adult

      Friendship takes hours more than charm. One study estimated about fifty hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, and around two hundred to become close27. That's why school and work make friends so easily: you see the same people constantly. As an adult you have to build that in. Join something that repeats weekly, like a class, a club, a volunteering shift or a regular quiz night, and go at least four or five times before deciding.

      Then be the one who suggests things. Most people are waiting for someone else to go first. "A few of us are getting food after this, want to come?" feels exposing to say and nearly always goes down fine.

      Old friends are often easier than new ones. "Thought of you the other day and realised it's been ages. How are you?" doesn't need a reason or an apology for the gap.

      Small contact helps

      Brief chats with people you barely know, like the person at the café counter or a neighbour, give a measurable lift to mood and sense of belonging28. If talking is too much right now, being around people still helps: work from a library, sit in a park. Pets, volunteering, faith communities and online groups are all real connection. If you want someone to talk to now, 7 Cups has free volunteer listeners, and the resources page has more.

      If someone in your life controls you, puts you down, cuts you off from people, frightens you or hurts you, that's abuse, and help exists. In the US, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline. In the UK, Refuge runs a free 24-hour line on 0808 2000 247. Elsewhere, try Find A Helpline. The Quick exit button leaves this site immediately.

      Grief

      Grief doesn't come in neat stages. The famous five stages came from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's work with people who were themselves dying, and they were never meant as a checklist for the bereaved. People also grieve pets, relationships, their health, jobs, homes and futures they'd planned. Numbness, anger, guilt, relief and laughing at the wrong moment are all common.

      Lauren Herschel described grief as a ball in a box with a pain button on one wall. At first the ball is huge and hits the button constantly. Over time the ball shrinks and hits it less often, but when it does, it hurts as much as it did on day one. A song or an anniversary can set it off out of nowhere. That isn't going backwards.

      Two modes

      Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut found that people who cope well with bereavement swing between two modes29. Some of the time they face the loss: crying, looking at photos, thinking about the person. The rest of the time they deal with practical changes or take a break from grief altogether. Both are healthy. A good afternoon isn't a betrayal. If you've avoided the grief completely for months, though, it might be time to let some of it in.

      Staying connected

      The old advice was to let go and move on. Most grief researchers now think keeping a bond with the person is normal and often healthy30. Talk to them, write to them, cook their recipes, keep their things. Say their name and ask other people to. Many bereaved people find the silence around the person harder than the reminders.

      Practical stuff

      • Plan ahead for the first birthday, anniversary and holidays. Decide whether you want company or quiet, and tell people.
      • Give people specific jobs. "Can you do my shopping on Thursdays?" is easier to say yes to than "let me know if you need anything".
      • Put off big decisions, like moving house, if you can.
      • Keep eating and sleeping. Grief is physically exhausting.
      • Try writing about the loss for fifteen or twenty minutes on a few days in a row. This kind of writing has been linked with better health in a range of studies31.
      • Find people who get it. Grief groups run online and in person, and What's Your Grief is a good place to start reading.

      Some losses get little recognition from the people around you: a miscarriage, a pet, an ex, an online friend, someone you had a complicated relationship with, someone still alive but changed by illness. Kenneth Doka called this "disenfranchised grief"32. It's just as real. You may have to look harder for people who take it seriously.

      Prolonged grief disorder became an official diagnosis in 202233. It covers grief that's still intense and disabling a year or more after a death (six months for children). If that sounds familiar, or you feel you can't go on without them, talk to someone. Grief counselling helps. In the UK, Cruse runs a free helpline.

      How you talk to yourself

      Most people have an inner voice that talks to them in a way they'd never put up with from anyone else. It gets louder and less accurate when you're tired, stressed or low. Aaron Beck built cognitive therapy on the observation that depressed patients' thoughts followed predictable, distorted patterns34. Learning to spot the patterns is most of the work.

      Common thinking traps

      Mind reading
      "They think I'm annoying." You don't know what they think. What else could explain how they acted?
      Catastrophising
      "If this goes wrong, everything's ruined." What's most likely to happen? If the worst did happen, how would I handle it?
      All or nothing
      "If it isn't perfect, I've failed." Most things sit somewhere in between. Doing part of it still moved it forward.
      Labelling
      "I'm such an idiot." I made a mistake. That's something I did, not a description of me.
      "Should" statements
      "I should be over this by now." According to whom? I'd like to feel better, and I'm working on it.
      Mental filter
      "The whole day was awful." One part was awful. Was anything else fine?
      Discounting the positive
      "They only said that to be nice." Maybe. Or they meant it.
      Personalising
      "It's my fault they're upset." Their mood has plenty of causes, and most of them aren't me.
      Emotional reasoning
      "I feel like a failure, so I must be one." The feeling is real. It still isn't evidence.

      Doing a thought record

      When one thought keeps stinging, write it out:

      1. The situation, in a sentence. Who, what, when.
      2. The feeling, and how strong it is out of 100.
      3. The thought, word for word. Pick the one that hurts most.
      4. Evidence for the thought. Facts only, the kind that would hold up in court.
      5. Evidence against it.
      6. A more balanced thought that accounts for both. Then re-rate the feeling.

      You're aiming for fairer, not cheerful. A drop from 90 to 60 is a good result. Get Self Help has free worksheets.

      Getting distance from a thought

      Two quick tricks for when you're too close to a thought to argue with it. From acceptance and commitment therapy: put "I'm having the thought that" in front of it35. "I'm a failure" becomes "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure". Same words, looser grip. The second: talk to yourself in the third person, by name. In experiments, people who thought through a stressful situation that way ("Why is Sam nervous about this?") were less anxious and performed better than people who used "I"36.

      Self-compassion

      People worry that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. In one set of experiments, people encouraged to be self-compassionate about a failure studied longer for a retest than people given a self-esteem boost or nothing37. Harsh self-criticism tends to make people avoid whatever they failed at.

      Kristin Neff, who's done much of the research, suggests a short exercise for bad moments38: hand on your chest, and tell yourself "this is hard right now, other people feel this too, and I can be kind to myself". It's awkward at first. If that's too much, write down exactly what your inner critic is saying, imagine a close friend saying it about themselves, and write what you'd say back to them.

      Anger and overwhelming emotions

      Some feelings arrive at full volume: rage, shame, panic, despair. At that level the thinking part of your brain is mostly offline, so reasoning your way out rarely works. You need a plan for the next few minutes that stops you making things worse.

      Anger is useful information. It usually means a line's been crossed or a need isn't being met. The goal is to stop it making your decisions.

      Change your body first

      Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) uses a set of skills called TIPP for emotions too strong to think through39:

      • Temperature: put your face in cold water for thirty seconds while holding your breath, or hold an ice pack over your eyes and cheeks. It triggers the diving reflex and slows your heart fast. Check with a doctor first if you have a heart condition or an eating disorder.
      • Intense exercise for five to ten minutes, like running up the stairs or star jumps, to burn off adrenaline.
      • Paced breathing, out-breath longer than in-breath, around five or six breaths a minute.
      • Paired muscle relaxation: tense a muscle group as you breathe in, let it go as you breathe out.

      Then decide what to do

      Another DBT skill is just to stop. Freeze, step back, notice what's happening inside and around you, and ask what the calm version of you would do. Don't send the message or make the call until it's passed. Write it if you need to and send it tomorrow, if you still want to.

      Punching cushions or "letting it all out" sounds like it should help, but in experiments venting tends to make people more aggressive afterwards40. Calming down works better.

      If arguments with a partner or family member keep escalating, agree a time-out signal while you're both calm. When either of you uses it, you separate for at least twenty minutes, which is about how long the body takes to settle after a surge of stress hormones41. Spend it doing something calming, not rehearsing your case, then come back to it.

      Urges

      For urges to shout, drink, self-harm or anything else, try urge surfing. Notice where you feel the urge in your body and watch it rise, peak and fall. Most fade within twenty or thirty minutes if you don't act on them, and each one you ride out makes the next a bit weaker.

      Afterwards

      Once you've calmed down, work out what was underneath. Anger often covers something like hurt, fear, embarrassment or feeling powerless. Knowing what you needed makes it easier to ask for next time, ideally starting from your side: "I felt dismissed when the plans changed without anyone asking me" lands better than "you always ignore me". DBT Self Help goes into these skills in more depth.

      Get help if anger is damaging your relationships, you're breaking things or scaring people, or you feel out of control. It's one of the most common reasons people start therapy, and it responds well.

      Trauma

      Trauma is as much about what an event did to you as the event itself, especially how your body learned to keep protecting you after the danger was over.

      Under threat, we react automatically. We fight, run, freeze, or fawn (try to keep the dangerous person happy). Nobody picks which one happens. If you froze or went along with something, that was your nervous system, and it's nothing to be ashamed of.

      Most people have strong reactions for a few weeks after something terrible, and most recover gradually without treatment. For some, the reactions stay switched on: flashbacks, nightmares, being easily startled, scanning rooms for danger, avoiding reminders, feeling numb or unreal, or reacting to small things with a force that surprises you. If that lasts more than about a month, it may be PTSD. Long or repeated trauma, especially in childhood, can lead to complex PTSD, which adds long-term difficulty with emotions, self-worth and relationships.

      Coming back to the present

      When you're pulled into the past, look slowly around the room and let your eyes land on things: colours, light, where the door is. Say the date and where you are out loud. Humming, pressure from a weighted blanket or a firm hug, and pushing your hands against a wall can all help. Keep something with a strong texture or smell in your pocket for when it happens out of the house. The exercise below helps too.

      5-4-3-2-1 grounding

      Use your senses to come back to the room. Tap a circle for each thing you notice. It moves on when you've found them all.

      In the meantime

      A nervous system that doesn't feel safe does better with routine: regular meals, regular sleep, predictable days. Be careful with alcohol and drugs. They're very commonly used to blunt trauma symptoms and tend to make them worse over time. Keep a note of your triggers, but don't try to avoid all of them, because avoidance shrinks your life and keeps the fear fresh. Tell at least one person you trust what you're dealing with, even without the details.

      Treatment

      Trauma-focused CBT and EMDR both have strong evidence for PTSD and are recommended in clinical guidelines42. The same guidelines advise against single-session "debriefing" straight after a traumatic event, which used to be routine and turned out not to help. A good trauma therapist works at a pace you can manage and makes sure you have ways to steady yourself before going near the worst memories. It's fine to ask a therapist what trauma training they have and how they'd approach your situation.

      If you're in danger now or someone is hurting you, use the crisis lines. For sexual assault in the US, RAINN runs a 24-hour hotline on 800-656-4673.

      Suicidal thoughts and self-harm

      If you're thinking about suicide or hurting yourself, you're in a lot of pain. Lots of people have had these thoughts and later felt better in ways they couldn't have imagined at the time. The most intense urges usually pass. The priority is getting through the next few hours safely.

      If you're thinking about suicide right now

      1. Put distance between yourself and anything you could use to hurt yourself. Give it to someone, lock it away, or leave the room or the building. Reducing access to means is one of the best-supported suicide prevention measures there is43.
      2. Don't stay alone if you can avoid it. Go where people are, or call someone and ask them to stay on the line.
      3. Contact a crisis line. Text if talking is too hard.
      4. Stay away from alcohol and drugs tonight. They make impulses harder to resist.
      5. Agree with yourself to wait. Just tonight, or just the next hour. Nothing needs deciding now.

      Suicidal crises are often shorter than they feel. A follow-up of more than 500 people who were stopped from jumping from the Golden Gate Bridge found that around 94% were still alive decades later or had died of natural causes44. Getting through the crisis bought them time, and time changed things.

      Lots of people don't call because they don't want to be a bother. Crisis lines exist for this exact situation, and they'd rather hear from you early than late. The people in your life would almost certainly rather know too.

      What happens when you call

      A trained person answers and listens. They'll probably ask how you're feeling and whether you're safe right now. They ask directly because it helps, and they won't judge you. Most calls end with talking it through and maybe making a plan. In most places, emergency services only get involved if someone is in immediate danger and can't keep themselves safe, and even then the counsellor will usually try to work it out with you first. Most lines let you stay anonymous, and you can hang up whenever you want.

      Make a safety plan

      The most useful thing to do on a steadier day is write a safety plan: a short list of your warning signs, things that help, people to contact and ways to make your surroundings safer. In a large study of people seen in US emergency departments after a suicidal crisis, those who made a safety plan and got follow-up calls had about 45% less suicidal behaviour over the next six months45. There's one you can fill in at the end of this page, and the free Stay Alive app can store one on your phone.

      Self-harm

      Self-harm is usually a way of coping with feelings that seem unbearable. Clinical guidelines are clear that people who self-harm should be treated with respect and offered proper psychological support46. You deserve ways of coping that don't hurt you.

      When the urge comes, delay it with a fifteen-minute timer, then another. Different alternatives suit different needs, so work out what self-harm usually does for you:

      • To feel something: a strong but safe sensation, like holding ice, a cold shower, something very sour or spicy, or drawing on your skin with a red pen.
      • To release anger or tension: something fast and physical, like sprinting, ripping up paper or screaming into a pillow.
      • For comfort: a blanket, a pet, a bath, a familiar film.
      • To stop feeling: something absorbing, like a game, a puzzle, loud music or talking to someone.

      The free Calm Harm app is built around this approach. If you do hurt yourself, clean the wound and keep a first aid kit so you can look after it properly.

      If a wound is deep, gaping or still bleeding after ten minutes of firm pressure, or if you've taken anything (even if you feel fine, because some substances cause delayed harm), get medical help now. Emergency staff are there to treat you.

      Phones and the internet

      The internet is where a lot of people find their community, and plenty of it is good. It's also built by well-paid teams whose job is to keep you scrolling. Total screen time matters less than whether you come away feeling better or worse, so pay attention to that.

      Change your setup

      Willpower loses against apps designed to beat it, so make the habit harder instead. Even having your phone on the desk, silenced and face down, seems to use up some of your attention; in one study people did better on memory and reasoning tests with their phone in another room47. Some changes that help:

      • Turn on greyscale. Apps are much less appealing without their colours.
      • Move the apps that hook you off your home screen, or delete them and use the clunkier browser version.
      • Turn off every notification that isn't from a real person.
      • Charge your phone outside the bedroom and get a cheap alarm clock.
      • Add a pause before opening apps. In a study of one sec, which makes you take a breath first, people opened their target apps much less often48. DetoxDroid does something similar on Android.

      Social media

      In one experiment, students who cut down to about ten minutes per platform per day for three weeks reported less loneliness and depression than a group who carried on as normal49. You don't have to quit, especially if your friends are there. Try a limit for two weeks and see how you feel.

      Mute or unfollow accounts that leave you feeling worse, friends included; muting is invisible. With the news, pick one or two set times a day and one trusted source, and keep it out of the first hour after waking and the last hour before bed.

      Drinking, drugs and habits

      Most people who drink too much, use drugs, gamble or get stuck in compulsive habits are trying to feel better or feel less. Over time it usually costs more than it gives. Addiction is a health problem, you don't have to hit rock bottom before changing, cutting down still matters, and slips are a normal part of recovery.

      Taking stock

      Some honest questions: am I using this to cope with feelings or get through the day? Have I tried to cut down and struggled? Is it affecting my health, money, work or relationships? Do I hide how much I use? For alcohol, the WHO's AUDIT questionnaire is a ten-question check that takes a couple of minutes50. For reference, UK guidelines say no more than 14 units a week (about six pints of average beer or six medium glasses of wine), spread over several days. US guidance is up to two drinks a day for men and one for women.

      Changing it

      1. Track it honestly for a week, without judging yourself: when, where, who with, how much and what mood.
      2. Look for the triggers. Common ones are times of day, certain people, stress, boredom and the HALT states (hungry, angry, lonely, tired).
      3. Write down your reasons to change and your reasons not to. Being honest about what you get out of it makes the plan more realistic.
      4. Plan something specific for the riskiest moments: "When I get in from work, I'll have a cold drink and a twenty-minute walk before I sit down."
      5. Make it harder to get. Don't keep it in the house, delete the betting apps, block gambling sites (GAMSTOP covers UK sites), change your route home.
      6. Get support. SMART Recovery runs free meetings based on practical tools, and AA and NA meet almost everywhere.

      Cravings work like the urges in the anger section: they rise, peak and fade within about half an hour if you don't feed them. Urge surfing, calling someone or leaving the situation all get you through.

      Stopping alcohol suddenly after heavy, regular drinking can cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures, and so can stopping benzodiazepines. Talk to a doctor; medically assisted withdrawal is standard and effective51. If you use drugs, never use alone, test what you can, start low, and learn the signs of overdose. TripSit has a live chat if you or a friend need help now.

      Trackers and support for gambling and porn are on the resources page.

      Identity and fitting in

      Some mental strain comes from a world that hasn't made room for you. If you're LGBTQ+, neurodivergent, disabled, a person of colour, an immigrant or otherwise outside the default, part of what you're carrying may be that. Psychologist Ilan Meyer called it minority stress52: the extra load of dealing with prejudice, expecting rejection and hiding parts of yourself.

      LGBTQ+

      LGBTQ+ people have higher rates of anxiety and depression because of stigma, rejection and stress. Acceptance makes a measurable difference: in a large Trevor Project survey, LGBTQ+ young people with at least one accepting adult were about 40% less likely to report a suicide attempt in the past year53. If you're an adult in a young person's life, you can be that person.

      If you're questioning, you don't need to have it figured out. Whether and when you come out is your call. Coming out to one safe person first is a reasonable way to test it. If home isn't safe, wait until you have somewhere else to go. The Trevor Project and TrevorSpace are for young people. The Gender Dysphoria Bible explains dysphoria thoroughly, and the resources page has trans health links gathered by FMHY.

      ADHD, autism and other neurodivergence

      Plenty of adults, especially women and people of colour, find out they're autistic or have ADHD much later in life, often after years of being called "too much" or lazy. Hiding your traits to fit in (masking, or camouflaging) is exhausting54, and in autistic adults it's been linked with higher suicide risk55. Having places where you can stop masking matters.

      Things that help a lot of neurodivergent people:

      • Body doubling: doing a task alongside someone, in person or on a video call. It sounds pointless and often makes a dull task possible.
      • Getting things out of your head: timers, lists on the wall, labelled alarms, and keeping things where you'll see them.
      • Breaking tasks into the first physical step. "Do taxes" is impossible. "Open the laptop and find last year's form" isn't.
      • Looking after your senses: noise-cancelling headphones, soft clothes, dim light, stimming freely.
      • Scheduling recovery time after social events or busy days, before you discover you needed it.

      How to ADHD, Embrace Autism and the Autistic Self Advocacy Network are good places to learn more. A formal assessment can get you support and adjustments, although waiting lists are long in a lot of places.

      Race, culture and belonging

      Racism and discrimination take a measurable toll on mental health, and it's tiring to explain your experience to a therapist who doesn't get it. Directories like Inclusive Therapists and Therapy for Black Girls make it easier to find someone who does. It's fine to ask a therapist directly how they work with clients from your background.

      Money, work and practical problems

      Sometimes the problem is the rent, the job, the debt or the exam. Breathing exercises won't fix an eviction notice, but practical help exists, and using it is part of looking after your mental health. People in debt are much more likely to have depression and other mental health problems56, and it works both ways.

      When money is frightening

      1. Pick a calm moment and open the post. Just open it and sort it into piles.
      2. Write down roughly what comes in each month and what goes out. Use bank statements, not memory.
      3. Separate priority debts from the rest. Rent or mortgage, energy, council tax or local taxes, court fines and child support come first, because the consequences of not paying are worst. Credit cards, overdrafts and loans from friends come after.
      4. Contact the people you owe early. Many have hardship arrangements, especially if you mention your mental health, and it's far easier to agree a plan before anything goes to court.
      5. Check you're getting every benefit, discount and grant you're entitled to. Many people aren't.
      6. Avoid payday loans, loan sharks and anyone charging to make debt disappear.

      Free advice is out there. In the US, dial 211 for local help, or try a non-profit credit counsellor through the NFCC. In the UK, Citizens Advice and StepChange are free. In England and Wales, if you're getting mental health crisis treatment, the Breathing Space scheme can freeze interest and creditor action while you're unwell. A debt adviser can apply for you.

      Work and study

      In many countries, mental health conditions are covered by disability law, such as the Equality Act in the UK and the Americans with Disabilities Act in the US. That can entitle you to reasonable adjustments: flexible hours, a quieter workspace, more breaks, time off for appointments, written instructions. You don't have to tell colleagues your diagnosis to ask. In the UK, the government's Access to Work scheme also offers free mental health support at work.

      Check whether your employer or college offers free counselling or an Employee Assistance Programme. A lot of people have one and don't know. Time off with a doctor's note for mental health is as legitimate as for anything else. Students should talk to a tutor or the disability service early, because extensions exist for exactly this.

      If you're a carer, look for local carers' organisations, carer's assessments and respite services. Carers burn out quietly, and your needs matter too.

      Getting professional help

      In most places, a GP or family doctor is the first step. Before the appointment, write down what you've been feeling, for how long, what it's affecting and anything you've tried. Appointments are short and it's easy to go blank. You can bring someone with you. If you don't know how to start, borrow this:

      "I've been struggling with my mental health. For about [how long] I've been feeling [low, anxious, numb, not sleeping]. It's affecting my [work, sleep, relationships, eating]. I'd like to talk about what support is available."

      Read it out or hand them the paper. If a doctor is dismissive, ask to see someone else.

      Kinds of therapy

      Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is structured and practical, and looks at how thoughts, feelings and behaviour feed each other. It has strong evidence for anxiety, depression, OCD and insomnia. Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) teaches skills for intense emotions, relationships and distress, and is often used for self-harm and borderline personality disorder. EMDR helps the brain process traumatic memories so they feel less raw. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) is about making room for difficult feelings while doing what matters to you. Interpersonal therapy focuses on relationships and life changes. Psychodynamic and person-centred counselling are more open-ended and explore your history and patterns at your own pace. Group therapy and peer support groups are often free or cheap.

      The relationship with your therapist matters a lot. Across hundreds of studies, a good working relationship is one of the most consistent predictors of how well therapy goes57. Trying one or two therapists before finding the right one is common.

      Questions to ask a therapist

      • What's your training, and who are you registered or licensed with?
      • Have you worked with people dealing with what I'm dealing with?
      • What would sessions look like, and how would we know if it's working?
      • How much does it cost, how long are sessions, and what's the cancellation policy?

      Good signs: you feel listened to, they explain what they're doing, and they take feedback. Bad signs: they talk about themselves a lot, push you into things you're not ready for, brush off your concerns, or blur boundaries (suggesting friendship or anything romantic). You can stop seeing a therapist at any point.

      Finding one

      In England you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for free without a GP. In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, start with your GP. In the US, check your insurer's directory, search Psychology Today, look at Open Path Collective for sliding-scale fees, or use FindTreatment.gov. University training clinics are cheaper and use supervised trainees. In Australia, ask your GP about a Mental Health Treatment Plan for Medicare-subsidised sessions. In Canada, look at provincial programmes, community clinics and workplace assistance programmes.

      Waiting lists can be long, so use the wait. Work through a free CBT workbook from the Centre for Clinical Interventions, try an online course like MoodGYM or This Way Up, and keep a mood log to bring to your first session.

      Medication

      Psychiatric medication helps a lot of people. A 2018 analysis of over 500 trials found all 21 antidepressants studied worked better than placebo for adults with major depression, though by varying amounts58. Medication plus therapy tends to beat either alone, especially for moderate to severe depression59.

      Before you start:

      • Many antidepressants take two to six weeks to work fully. Side effects often show up first and ease off.
      • The first one may not suit you. That's common, and there are plenty of others.
      • Don't stop suddenly without talking to your doctor. Tapering slowly, sometimes over months, cuts withdrawal symptoms60.
      • Tell your doctor about other medication, supplements like St John's Wort, alcohol and recreational drugs. Some combinations are dangerous.
      • If you feel worse, more agitated or have new suicidal thoughts after starting or changing a dose, tell your doctor straight away, particularly if you're under 25.

      When someone you love is struggling

      People often go quiet around someone who's struggling because they're scared of saying the wrong thing. You don't need perfect words. Showing up and staying around matters more.

      What to say

      Listen more than you fix. Reflect back what you've heard ("so work's been relentless and you can't switch off at night") and let them correct you. Validate the feeling even if you'd see the situation differently: "that sounds really hard" goes a long way. Ask whether they want you to listen or help solve something, because getting that wrong is the most common way support misfires.

      Avoid "think positive", "other people have it worse" and unsolicited yoga recommendations. Don't make it about you, and don't get angry that they didn't tell you sooner. The most common mistake of all is disappearing because you don't know what to say.

      What to do

      Offer specific help. "Let me know if you need anything" hands the work back to them. "I'm going to the shop, what can I get you?", "Can I come to the appointment with you?" or "I'll pick you up for a walk on Saturday at ten" are much easier to accept. Keep checking in, even if replies are short or don't come. Encourage professional help without nagging, and offer to help find a therapist or sit with them while they make the call.

      If you're worried about suicide

      Ask directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Use the word. Research has found no evidence that asking increases suicidal thoughts, and some evidence it reduces them61. If they say yes, listen without arguing or panicking. Ask if they've thought about how and whether they have the means. Help them put distance between themselves and anything they could use. Help them reach support: a crisis line you call together, their doctor, or emergency services if they're in immediate danger. Follow up over the next few days. Don't promise to keep it secret if their life is at risk.

      Looking after yourself

      Supporting someone is draining, and you're a friend or family member, not their therapist. Set limits on what you can offer, share the load with other people in their life, and make sure you've got someone to talk to too. NAMI runs free programmes for families, and Movember's Conversations tool lets you rehearse a difficult conversation. A Mental Health First Aid course is worth doing if there's one near you.

      A plan for bad days

      When you're in a bad place it's hard to think of what to do, so you write it down beforehand. That's a safety plan. Fill this in on a steadier day. It saves as you type, only to this browser on this device, and nothing is sent anywhere.

      Thoughts, moods or behaviours that tell me I'm starting to slip.
      Things that calm or distract me, even a little.
      Where I can go and who I can be around without having to explain.
      Names and phone numbers.
      My doctor, therapist, and the crisis numbers for where I live.
      Things I can remove, or put out of reach, when I'm in a bad place.
      People, animals, plans, things I look forward to.

      When it's done, take a photo or keep a printed copy somewhere you'll find it, and think about giving a copy to someone you trust.
