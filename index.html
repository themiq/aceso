    A guide for when things are hard

    Everything I'd want someone to tell me about looking after my mind, written down in one place. Read the parts you need and skip the rest.

    Last updated November 2026 · about a 45 minute read in full

    If you might not be safe tonight

    Please stop reading and contact someone now. Call your local emergency number (911, 999, 112, 000 or 111, depending on where you are) or one of the free crisis lines listed below. In the US you can call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland you can call Samaritans on 116 123. They're free, and you don't need to be in any particular state to call.

    Contents

      I made Aceso because the advice that helps people through a bad stretch is scattered all over the internet, and most of it is either too clinical to read when you're struggling or too vague to use. This page tries to sit between those. It's long because there's a lot to cover. Each section stands on its own, so jump to whatever is closest to what you're dealing with.

      A few things up front. I'm not your doctor, and nothing here can diagnose you or replace proper care. What I can do is explain what tends to be going on, pass along the things that have good evidence behind them, and point you to people who can help properly. The resources page has around 150 links, including apps, workbooks, helplines and communities, sorted by topic.

      Nothing you type on this site leaves your browser. There are no accounts, no analytics and no cookies. If you need to leave in a hurry, the Quick exit button in the corner (or pressing Esc three times) takes you to a weather search and replaces this page in your history.

      Where to start

      Everyone has mental health in the same way everyone has physical health. It goes up and down. Some weeks you're fine, some weeks you've got the equivalent of a bad cold, and some stretches of life need real treatment. About one in five adults has a diagnosable mental health condition in any given year, so if you're struggling, you're in very ordinary company.

      The model I find most useful is the stress bucket. Picture everything stressful in your life pouring into a bucket: work, money, health, relationships, bad sleep, the news, old things you never dealt with. People are handed different sized buckets. Genetics and early life play a part in that, and none of it is your fault. When the bucket overflows, you struggle, often over something small that looks like it came out of nowhere. It didn't. The bucket was already full.

      Buckets have taps, though. Rest, other people, exercise, therapy, saying no, doing something you enjoy: these let some of the water out. Most of this guide is about two things, putting less in and letting more out.

      Too wired, or too flat

      Psychologists talk about a "window of tolerance", the state where you can cope with what's in front of you. Above it you're keyed up: anxious, panicky, irritable, unable to sit still. Below it you shut down and feel numb, foggy and far away. Stress and trauma make the window narrower. If you're above it, things that slow your body down help most, such as long out-breaths, cold water on your face, a slow walk or dim light. If you're below it, gentle stimulation works better: moving around, music, a strong taste like a mint or something sour, cold air, or talking to someone.

      Name what you're feeling

      Putting a feeling into words tends to take some of the heat out of it. Researchers call this affect labelling. Precise words work better than vague ones, so "disappointed and a bit embarrassed" beats "bad". If you struggle to find the words, the free How We Feel app gives you a long list to choose from.

      Anxiety and panic

      Anxiety is your body's alarm system. It's meant to keep you alive, and when it fires it floods you with adrenaline so you can run or fight. That's useful when there's a real threat. The trouble is that the alarm can become oversensitive, going off over an email or a phone call, or ringing for weeks with no obvious cause.

      It can show up as a racing heart, tight chest, shallow breathing, a churning stomach, or thoughts that loop on "what if". Some people feel detached from themselves or as though the world isn't quite real. That last one is called derealisation, and although it's unsettling, it's harmless and passes. Caffeine, poor sleep, hunger, alcohol the day before, and stress you haven't dealt with all make the alarm more sensitive.

      During a panic attack

      The single most useful thing to know is that a panic attack can't hurt you. It feels like you might faint, have a heart attack, or lose your mind, and none of those happen. Adrenaline peaks and falls, usually within ten or twenty minutes. Fighting the panic tends to feed it, so try to let it run its course while you do the following:

      1. Tell yourself what it is: "This is a panic attack. It's horrible and it will pass."
      2. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in. In for four, out for six. The exercise below will pace you.
      3. Put your feet flat on the floor and press your palms together. Name a few things you can see.
      4. If you can, splash cold water on your face or hold something cold. It slows your heart rate.

      A breathing exercise

      Breathe in while the circle grows and out while it shrinks. Six or more rounds is a good start. The 4 and 6 pattern suits most people; box breathing and 4-7-8 are there if you already know them.

      For everyday worry

      Avoiding the things that frighten you brings relief in the moment and makes the fear bigger over time. The best-evidenced treatment for most anxiety is gradual exposure: facing the feared thing in small, manageable steps. If phone calls terrify you, you might start by listening to a voicemail, then call a recorded information line, then ring a shop to ask its opening hours. Stay with each step until the anxiety drops a little before moving on.

      Worry time is another approach that works better than it sounds. Give yourself a fifteen minute slot each day to worry on purpose. When a worry turns up at another time, write it down and leave it for later. When the slot comes, many of the items will have shrunk. For the rest, ask whether there's anything you can do about it. If there is, plan one small step. If there isn't, practise noticing the thought and letting it pass.

      It can also help to follow a worry all the way to the end. "I'll mess up the presentation." Then what? "People will think I'm bad at my job." Then what? The worst case usually turns out to be unpleasant and survivable, and seeing that takes some of the pressure off.

      Talk to a doctor if anxiety is stopping you doing things you need or want to do, if you're having regular panic attacks, or if you're avoiding more and more of your life. Generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, OCD and health anxiety all respond well to CBT, and sometimes medication. The free CBT workbooks from the Centre for Clinical Interventions are the best self-help material I know of. More on the resources page.

      Depression and low mood

      Depression often feels less like sadness than like heaviness. Everything takes more effort, things you loved go grey, and you feel tired no matter how much you sleep. It can bring guilt, a sense of being a burden, trouble thinking clearly, changes in appetite, and thoughts about death.

      Depression also lies to you. It says this is how things will always be, that nobody would care, that you're the problem. Those thoughts feel like insight. They're symptoms, and they lift as the depression does.

      Everyone has low days, particularly after a loss or during winter. If you've felt low for most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or longer, and it's affecting your life, it's worth seeing a doctor. You won't be wasting their time. A free screening tool like the one from Mental Health America can help you put it into words before the appointment.

      What helps

      With depression, waiting until you feel like doing something doesn't work, because the feeling doesn't come. Action has to come first and the motivation follows later, if at all. This is the idea behind behavioural activation, one of the most effective treatments for depression. Each day, plan one small thing that gives you a sense of achievement (washing a mug counts) and one small thing that might give you a flicker of pleasure, like sitting in the sun for five minutes. Do them whether you want to or not, and notice afterwards whether anything shifted, even slightly.

      On the worst days, lower the bar to the floor. Eat something, drink some water, take any medication you're on, get a bit of daylight, and get to bedtime. When you're depressed, that's a full day's work. If a shower is too much, brush your teeth and change your shirt. If washing up is too much, use paper plates for a week. KC Davis's book How to Keep House While Drowning is very good on this.

      Morning daylight and a short daily walk both have decent evidence behind them for mood. You don't need a gym or a running habit; ten minutes outside is enough to start.

      If your mood drops every autumn and lifts in spring, you may have seasonal affective disorder. A 10,000 lux light box used for twenty to thirty minutes each morning helps many people, and a doctor can advise on it. Depression and anxiety after having a baby are common too, affecting up to one in seven new parents, and Postpartum Support International runs a helpline and free groups.

      If you're having thoughts of suicide, can't manage basic care, or it's been going on for weeks, please reach out now. Depression is very treatable with therapy, medication or both, and a GP or family doctor is a good first step. The section on suicidal thoughts is further down.

      Stress and burnout

      Stress is having too much to deal with. Burnout is what happens after months of it, when there's nothing left. The usual signs are exhaustion, a cynical or detached feeling about work or caring, and a sense that nothing you do makes a difference. You might be getting ill more often, snapping at people, dreading things you used to handle easily, or needing more and more wine or scrolling to switch off. It happens to students, parents and carers as often as it happens to people with jobs.

      A long weekend won't fix months of overload. Recovery needs real rest and a change to whatever is draining you.

      Dealing with stress in your body

      In their book Burnout, Emily and Amelia Nagoski make a useful distinction. Dealing with the cause of stress (the deadline, the difficult person) is separate from dealing with the stress itself, which lives in your body. Even if the cause can't be fixed yet, your body needs a signal that the danger has passed. Physical activity is the most effective signal. Slow breathing, a proper laugh, a long hug, a good cry and making something with your hands all help as well.

      Taking things off the pile

      Write down everything you're responsible for at the moment. Next to each item, decide whether you must do it, could hand it to someone, could drop it, or could do it badly. Then pick one item to hand off or drop this week. Doing something adequately instead of perfectly is a legitimate choice.

      Saying no gets easier with a few stock phrases ready: "I don't have capacity for that right now." "Can I get back to you tomorrow?" "I can do this part but not that one." "That doesn't work for me" is a complete sentence and needs no explanation after it.

      It also helps to think about which kind of rest you're short of. Sleep is one kind. There's also rest from thinking, from noise and screens, from people, and from having to keep up appearances. Most burnt-out people are missing more than one.

      Burnout overlaps with depression, and with ADHD and autism, where years of masking wear people down. If proper rest doesn't help after a few weeks, see a doctor.

      Sleep

      Poor sleep makes everything harder to cope with, and struggling makes it harder to sleep. Improving sleep is one of the most effective things you can do for your mental health, and it's more fixable than it feels at 3am.

      If you only change one thing, get up at the same time every day, weekends included. A fixed wake time anchors your body clock, and the sleepiness at night follows from it. Get daylight in your eyes within an hour of waking, ideally outdoors. Keep caffeine to the morning, and give yourself an hour of dim light and low stimulation before bed. A cool, dark, quiet room helps. If your head fills with worries at night, write them down before you get into bed so your brain doesn't have to hold them.

      If you've been lying awake for around twenty minutes, get up. Sit somewhere dim, read something dull, and go back when your eyelids are heavy. It feels backwards. The point is to stop your brain learning that bed is where you lie awake and get frustrated. For a racing mind, try a "cognitive shuffle": pick a word, and for each letter imagine as many things as you can that start with it. It's boring enough to let you drift off. Rain sounds from Rainy Mood or a sleep story can do the same job.

      Short naps before mid-afternoon are fine. Long or late ones will cost you the night. Alcohol helps you fall asleep but makes the sleep shallow and broken, so you wake up more tired. Melatonin mostly helps with body-clock problems like jet lag and shift work rather than general insomnia, and sleeping pills are a short-term option to discuss with a doctor, never to mix with alcohol.

      CBT for insomnia (CBT-i) works better than medication over the long run. The US Department of Veterans Affairs makes a free app called CBT-i Coach that walks you through it, and anyone can use it.

      See a doctor if you've had insomnia for more than three months, if you snore loudly and wake up gasping (a possible sign of sleep apnoea), or if you're sleepy all day despite a full night in bed. There are more sleep guides on the resources page.

      Food, movement and daylight

      Going for a run won't cure depression, and anyone who tells you otherwise is oversimplifying. Your brain is still part of your body, though, and looking after the body is a slow, steady way of looking after your mood.

      Eating

      The main thing is regularity. Skipping meals drops your blood sugar, and low blood sugar feels a lot like anxiety, irritability or despair. Aim to eat something every three to five hours, with some protein and fibre where you can manage it. If cooking is beyond you right now, a plate of cheese, crackers, fruit and nuts is a meal. So is toast with peanut butter, instant noodles with a handful of frozen vegetables and an egg, or a microwave rice pouch with a tin of beans. Keep a few of these in the cupboard for bad days.

      If food has become complicated for you, whether that's restricting, bingeing, purging, over-exercising, or thoughts about your body taking up much of your day, please talk to someone. Eating disorders affect people of every size, age and gender. Beat in the UK and ANAD in the US both offer free support.

      Moving

      For mild to moderate depression, some studies find exercise works about as well as therapy or medication. The best kind is whatever you'll keep doing: walking, dancing in the kitchen, stretching, swimming, gardening, a beginner yoga video. Start smaller than seems worthwhile. Put your shoes on and walk to the end of the road. If you want to carry on, carry on, and if not, you still went. DAREBEE, Yoga With Adriene and the NHS Couch to 5K plan are all free.

      Getting outside

      Around two hours a week in nature is linked with better wellbeing, and parks and tree-lined streets count. Daylight also keeps your body clock steady, which helps both sleep and mood.

      Thyroid problems, low iron, low vitamin D, low B12, hormonal changes and many chronic illnesses can all cause low mood, anxiety or exhaustion. Ask your doctor for a basic blood test to rule them out.

      Loneliness

      You can be lonely in a crowd, in a relationship, or with hundreds of people in your phone. Loneliness has less to do with how many people are around you than with whether you feel known by any of them. It's very common, and it isn't a sign that something is wrong with you. Like hunger, it's a signal about a need.

      One cruel thing about loneliness is that it makes people more guarded and quicker to read rejection into neutral situations. That makes connecting harder just when you need it. Knowing this can help you push past the voice that says nobody wants to hear from you. Studies consistently find that people underestimate how much others appreciate being contacted.

      Small steps

      Low-stakes contact is easier to start with: a meme with "this made me think of you", asking how something went, a voice note, a photo. You don't need a reason to message someone.

      Most friendships form through seeing the same people again and again, so join something that repeats. A class, a volunteering shift, a running club, a choir, a board game night or a Discord server about something you care about will all do. Go a few times before deciding whether it's for you.

      If talking is too much at the moment, being near people still helps. Work from a library or café, go to a quiet museum, sit in a park. Pets, volunteering with animals, faith communities and online groups are all real sources of belonging. If you want someone to talk to now, 7 Cups has free volunteer listeners, and the resources page lists more.

      If someone in your life controls you, puts you down, cuts you off from other people, frightens you or hurts you, that isn't your fault, and help exists. In the US, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline. In the UK, Refuge runs a free 24-hour line on 0808 2000 247. Elsewhere, try Find A Helpline. The Quick exit button at the top leaves this site immediately.

      Grief

      Grief doesn't follow neat stages, it doesn't keep to a timetable, and it isn't only about death. People grieve pets, relationships, their health, jobs, homes, and futures they'd planned. Numbness, anger, guilt, relief and laughter at the wrong moment are all normal parts of it.

      One picture of grief I've found helpful is a ball in a box with a pain button on one wall. At first the ball is huge and presses the button constantly. Over time the box grows around it, so the ball hits the button less often. When it does, though, a song or a smell or an anniversary can make it hurt as much as it did on day one. That's normal, and it doesn't mean you've gone backwards.

      Some things that help: saying the person's name and telling stories about them, planning ahead for birthdays and holidays, keeping up the basics of food and sleep, writing them a letter, and finding other people who understand. Grief groups exist for this, online and in person. What's Your Grief is a good place to start reading.

      Some losses go unrecognised by the people around you: a miscarriage, a pet, an ex, an online friend, someone you had a complicated relationship with, or someone who is still alive but changed by illness. That grief is real too, and it deserves the same care.

      If many months on the grief is still so intense that you can't function, or you feel you can't go on without them, talk to someone. Prolonged grief is recognised and treatable, and grief counselling helps. In the UK, Cruse runs a free helpline.

      How you talk to yourself

      Most of us have an inner voice that talks to us in a way we'd never accept from anyone else. It's often trying, badly, to protect us, by getting in first with the criticism or keeping us from trying things that might go wrong. What it says isn't reliable, though, and it gets louder and less accurate when you're tired, stressed or low.

      Common thinking traps

      CBT calls these cognitive distortions. Everyone falls into them. Learning to spot them is most of the work.

      Mind reading
      "They think I'm annoying." You don't know what they think. What else could explain how they acted?
      Catastrophising
      "If this goes wrong, everything is ruined." What's most likely to happen? If the worst did happen, how would I get through it?
      All or nothing
      "If it isn't perfect, I've failed." Most things sit somewhere in between. Doing part of it still counts.
      Labelling
      "I'm such an idiot." I made a mistake. That's something I did, and it doesn't define me.
      "Should" statements
      "I should be over this by now." According to whom? I'd like to feel better, and I'm working on it.
      Mental filter
      "The whole day was awful." One part was awful. Was anything else okay, even something small?
      Personalising
      "It's my fault they're upset." Their feelings have plenty of causes, and most of them have nothing to do with me.
      Emotional reasoning
      "I feel like a failure, so I must be one." The feeling is real. It still isn't evidence.

      Self-compassion

      People sometimes worry that being kind to themselves will make them lazy or let them off the hook. The research says the opposite: people who treat themselves with compassion bounce back from setbacks faster and are more likely to try again. Kristin Neff, who has done much of that research, suggests a short exercise for hard moments. Put a hand on your chest and say to yourself, "This is really hard right now. Other people feel this too. May I be kind to myself." It feels awkward the first few times.

      Another way in is to write down exactly what your inner critic is saying, then imagine a close friend saying it about themselves. Write down what you'd say back to them, and read it to yourself.

      When a particular thought keeps stinging, a thought record helps. Write down the situation, the thought, the feeling and how strong it is out of 100, then the evidence for and against the thought, then a more balanced version. Rate the feeling again at the end. Get Self Help has free worksheets.

      Anger and overwhelming emotions

      Some feelings arrive like a wave: rage, shame, panic, despair. When they're at full strength, the thinking part of your brain more or less goes offline, so trying to reason your way out rarely works. What helps is having a plan for getting through the next few minutes without making things worse.

      Anger itself is fine. It usually means a line has been crossed or a need isn't being met. The aim is to stop it making your decisions for you.

      Change your body first

      Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) has a set of skills for emotions that are too high to think through. The first is temperature: cold water on your face for thirty seconds while holding your breath, or an ice pack over your eyes and cheeks, slows your heart rate quickly. The second is intense exercise for five to ten minutes, such as running up the stairs or doing star jumps, to burn off the adrenaline. Then slow your breathing so the out-breath is longer than the in-breath, and try tensing each muscle group as you breathe in and letting go as you breathe out.

      Another DBT skill is simply to stop. Freeze before you act, take a step back, notice what's going on inside you and around you, and then ask what the wisest version of you would do next. Don't send the message, make the call or leave the room in anger until the wave has passed. Write the message if you need to, just don't send it.

      When things have calmed down, it's worth asking what was underneath. Anger often guards something softer, such as hurt, fear, embarrassment or feeling powerless. Knowing what you needed makes it easier to ask for it next time. DBT Self Help covers these skills in depth.

      Get help if anger is damaging your relationships, if you're breaking things or frightening people, or if you feel out of control. Anger is one of the most common reasons people start therapy, and it responds well to it.

      Trauma

      Trauma is about what an event did to you as much as the event itself, and in particular about how your body learned to keep protecting you after the danger had gone.

      When we're threatened, we respond automatically. We fight, run, freeze, or fawn, meaning we try to keep the dangerous person happy. Nobody chooses which of these happens, so if you froze or went along with something, that was your nervous system doing its job, and you have nothing to be ashamed of. After trauma, those responses can stay switched on. You might have flashbacks or nightmares, startle easily, scan rooms for danger, avoid reminders, feel numb or unreal, or react to small things with a force that surprises you. These are your body's attempts to protect you, and they can be treated.

      Coming back to the present

      When you're pulled into the past, slowly look around the room and let your eyes land on things: colours, light, where the door is. It tells your brain you're here and now. Humming, pressure from a weighted blanket or a firm hug, and pushing your hands against a wall can all help. So can the 5-4-3-2-1 exercise below.

      5-4-3-2-1 grounding

      Use your senses to come back to the room. Tap a circle for each thing you notice. It moves on when you've found them all.

      You don't need to work through trauma alone, and you shouldn't have to dig into it without support. Trauma-focused CBT and EMDR both have strong evidence for PTSD, and a good trauma therapist will go at a pace that feels safe to you.

      If you're in danger now or someone is hurting you, use the crisis lines. For sexual assault in the US, RAINN runs a 24-hour hotline on 800-656-4673.

      Suicidal thoughts and self-harm

      If you're thinking about suicide or hurting yourself, you're in a great deal of pain, and that doesn't make you weak or broken. Many people have had these thoughts and gone on to feel better, often in ways they couldn't have imagined at the time. The most intense urges usually pass. What matters most is getting through the next few hours safely.

      If you're thinking about suicide right now

      1. Put distance between yourself and anything you could use to hurt yourself. Give it to someone, lock it away, or go to a different room. This saves lives, because the worst moments pass.
      2. Try not to be alone. Go where other people are, or call someone and ask them to stay on the line.
      3. Contact a crisis line. Text if talking is too hard.
      4. Avoid alcohol and drugs for now. They make impulses harder to resist.
      5. Agree with yourself to wait. Not forever, just tonight or the next hour. You don't have to decide anything now.

      A lot of people don't call because they don't want to be a bother. Crisis lines exist for exactly this, and the people who staff them would much rather hear from you early than late. The people in your life would almost certainly rather know, too.

      What happens when you call a crisis line

      A trained person answers and listens. They'll probably ask how you're feeling and whether you're safe right now. They ask directly because it helps, and they won't judge you or tell you off. Most calls end with talking things through and perhaps making a plan together. In most places emergency services only get involved if someone is in immediate danger and can't keep themselves safe, and even then the counsellor will usually try to work it out with you first. You can stay anonymous on most lines, and you can hang up whenever you like.

      Self-harm

      Self-harm is usually a way of coping with feelings that seem unbearable. It makes sense to reach for something that brings relief, and you deserve ways of coping that don't hurt you. If you feel the urge, try delaying it with a fifteen minute timer, then another. Strong but safe sensations can take the edge off: holding ice, a cold shower, eating something very sour or spicy, or drawing on your skin with a red pen. Some people need a release instead, like screaming into a pillow, ripping up paper or sprinting. Others need comfort, such as a blanket, a pet or a familiar programme. The free Calm Harm app is built around these techniques.

      If you've hurt yourself and the wound is deep or won't stop bleeding, or if you've taken anything (even if you feel fine, because some substances cause delayed harm), get medical help now. Emergency staff are there to treat you, not judge you.

      The single most useful thing you can do while you're feeling a bit steadier is to make a safety plan. There's one you can fill in at the end of this page, and the free Stay Alive app can hold one on your phone.

      Phones and the internet

      The internet can be a lifeline. It's where a lot of people find their community, and it has plenty of good things in it. It's also built by talented teams whose job is to keep you scrolling. Screen time as a number matters less than whether you come away feeling better or worse, and that's the thing to pay attention to.

      Willpower doesn't do well against apps designed by people paid to beat it, so change your surroundings instead. Turning your phone to greyscale makes it far less appealing. Move the apps that pull you in off the home screen, or log out after each use. Turn off every notification that isn't from a real person. Charge your phone outside the bedroom and buy a cheap alarm clock. Apps like one sec or DetoxDroid add a pause before an app opens, which is often enough to break the habit.

      Mute or unfollow accounts that leave you feeling worse about yourself, including friends. Muting is invisible. Follow people who make you laugh or teach you something instead.

      With the news, pick one or two set times a day and one trusted source, and keep it out of the first hour after you wake and the last hour before bed. You can care about the world and still protect your head.

      Drinking, drugs and habits

      Most people who drink too much, use drugs, gamble or get stuck in compulsive habits are trying to feel better, or to feel less. That makes sense. Over time, though, it usually costs more than it gives. Addiction is a health problem, and you don't have to hit rock bottom before you're allowed to change anything. Cutting down counts, and slips are a normal part of recovery.

      Some honest questions to ask yourself: am I using this to cope with feelings or to get through the day? Have I tried to cut down and found it hard? Is it affecting my health, money, work or relationships? Do I hide how much I use? If some of these ring true, treat it as information and nothing more damning than that.

      A good first step is tracking it honestly for a week without judging yourself, then looking at when, where, with whom and in what mood you use. Once you can see the triggers, you can plan something else for those moments. A sober month is a useful experiment. SMART Recovery runs free meetings based on practical, science-based tools, and AA and NA meet almost everywhere.

      Stopping alcohol or benzodiazepines suddenly after heavy, regular use can be dangerous and can cause seizures. Talk to a doctor about stopping safely. If you use drugs, never use alone, test what you can, and learn the signs of overdose. TripSit has a live chat if you or a friend need help now.

      More recovery tools, including trackers and support for gambling and pornography, are on the resources page.

      Identity and fitting in

      A lot of mental pain comes from a world that hasn't made room for who you are. If you're LGBTQ+, neurodivergent, disabled, a person of colour, an immigrant, or different in some other way, part of what you're carrying may be the strain of that. Researchers call it minority stress. Nothing is wrong with who you are.

      LGBTQ+

      LGBTQ+ people have higher rates of anxiety and depression because of stigma, rejection and stress, and their identity itself plays no part in that. For young people, having even one accepting adult significantly lowers the risk. If you're questioning, you don't need to have it worked out, and whether and when you come out is up to you. The Trevor Project and its community TrevorSpace are for young people. The Gender Dysphoria Bible is a thorough, kind explanation of dysphoria, and the resources page has a section of trans health resources gathered by FMHY.

      ADHD, autism and other neurodivergence

      Plenty of adults, especially women and people of colour, find out they're autistic or have ADHD much later in life, often after years of feeling "too much", lazy, or like everyone else got an instruction manual they didn't. Hiding your traits to fit in, known as masking, is exhausting and is linked to burnout and depression.

      Some things that help: doing tasks alongside someone else in person or on a video call (body doubling), getting things out of your head and onto timers, lists and alarms, and looking after your senses with headphones, soft clothes and dim light. It also helps to find places where you can stop masking. How to ADHD, Embrace Autism and the Autistic Self Advocacy Network are good places to learn more.

      Race, culture and belonging

      Racism and discrimination take a measurable toll on mental health. Many people want a therapist who shares or understands their background, and directories like Inclusive Therapists and Therapy for Black Girls make that easier to find.

      Money, work and practical problems

      Sometimes the problem isn't in your head at all. It's the rent, the job, the debt or the exam. Breathing exercises won't fix an eviction notice, but practical help exists, and getting it counts as looking after your mental health.

      When money is frightening

      Money worries and poor mental health feed each other, and it's not a personal failing to be caught in that loop. Pick a calm moment, make a drink, and open the post. Just open it and sort it into piles. Write down roughly what comes in and what goes out. Housing, energy, council tax or local rates, and food come first. Contact the people you owe early, because many have hardship arrangements, especially if you mention your mental health. Avoid payday loans and anyone promising a quick fix.

      Free, non-judgemental advice is available. In the US, dial 211 for local help, or try a non-profit credit counsellor through the NFCC. In the UK, Citizens Advice and StepChange are free.

      Work and study

      In many countries, mental health conditions are covered by disability law, which may entitle you to reasonable adjustments at work. Check whether your employer or college offers free counselling or an Employee Assistance Programme; a surprising number of people have one and don't know. Taking time off with a doctor's note for your mental health is as legitimate as taking it for anything else. Students should talk to a tutor or the disability service early, since extensions exist for exactly this.

      If you're caring for someone, look for local carers' organisations and respite services. Carers burn out without anyone noticing, and your needs count too.

      Getting professional help

      Asking for help is often the hardest part. In most places a GP or family doctor is a sensible first step. If you don't know what to say, you can borrow this:

      "I've been struggling with my mental health. For about [how long] I've been feeling [low, anxious, numb, not sleeping]. It's affecting my [work, sleep, relationships, eating]. I'd like to talk about what support is available."

      Write it down and read it out, or hand them the piece of paper. Doctors hear this every day. If one is dismissive, you're allowed to ask to see someone else.

      Kinds of therapy

      Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is structured and practical, looking at how thoughts, feelings and behaviour affect each other. It has strong evidence for anxiety, depression, OCD and insomnia. Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) teaches skills for intense emotions, relationships and distress, and is often used for self-harm and borderline personality disorder. EMDR helps the brain process traumatic memories so they feel less raw. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) works on making room for difficult feelings while moving towards what matters to you. Psychodynamic and person-centred counselling are more open-ended and look at your history and patterns at your own pace. Group therapy and peer support groups are often free or cheap, and hearing "me too" from other people helps in its own way.

      The relationship with the therapist matters more than the type of therapy. It's normal to try one or two before finding someone who fits.

      Finding it

      In England you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for free without seeing a GP; in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, start with your GP. In the US, check your insurer's directory, search Psychology Today, look at Open Path Collective for sliding-scale fees, or use FindTreatment.gov. University training clinics offer cheaper sessions with supervised trainees. In Australia, ask your GP about a Mental Health Treatment Plan, which gives you Medicare-subsidised sessions. In Canada, look at provincial programmes, community clinics and workplace assistance programmes. Anywhere, online CBT courses like MoodGYM and This Way Up are a reasonable place to start while you wait.

      Medication

      Antidepressants and other psychiatric medications change some people's lives. Taking them doesn't mean you've failed or taken the easy way out. A few things worth knowing: many take two to six weeks to work fully, and side effects often show up first and ease off later. The first one you try may not suit you, which is common and doesn't mean nothing will. Never stop suddenly without talking to your doctor, because tapering matters. Tell them about other medication, supplements such as St John's Wort, and anything else you take. For many people, medication and therapy together work better than either alone.

      When someone you love is struggling

      When someone you care about is having a hard time, it's easy to freeze for fear of saying the wrong thing. You don't need perfect words. Turning up and staying around matters far more.

      Listen more than you try to fix. "That sounds really hard" goes a long way. Ask whether they want you to listen or to help solve something. Offer practical help, like dropping off food, helping book an appointment or sitting with them while they make a call. Suggest a walk or a meal together. Keep checking in, even if the replies are short or don't come.

      Try not to say "think positive" or "other people have it worse", and hold back on suggestions like yoga unless they ask. Don't make it about you, and don't get angry that they didn't tell you sooner. And don't disappear because you don't know what to say, which is more common than any of the rest.

      If you're worried about suicide

      Ask them directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Use the word. Asking does not put the idea in anyone's head, and it shows you can handle the answer. Listen without arguing or panicking. Help them put distance between themselves and anything they could use to hurt themselves. Help them reach support, whether that's a crisis line you call together, their doctor, or emergency services if they're in immediate danger. Then follow up over the next few days. Don't promise to keep it secret if their life is at risk.

      Look after yourself as well. Supporting someone is draining, and you're their friend or family member, not their therapist. NAMI runs free programmes for families, and Movember's Conversations tool lets you practise a difficult conversation beforehand.

      A plan for bad days

      A safety plan is a short written list of what helps you when things get bad. Clinicians use them because they work: when you're struggling, it's hard to think of what to do, so you write it down beforehand. Fill this in on a day when you feel reasonably steady. It saves as you type, and it only saves to this browser on this device. Nothing is sent anywhere.

      Thoughts, moods or behaviours that tell me I'm starting to slip.
      Things that calm or distract me, even a little.
      Where I can go and who I can be around without having to explain.
      Names and phone numbers.
      My doctor, therapist, and the crisis numbers for where I live.
      Things I can remove, or put out of reach, when I'm in a bad place.
      People, animals, plans, small things I look forward to.

      Once it's done, take a photo of it or keep a printed copy somewhere easy to find, and consider sharing it with someone you trust.
