    A guide for when things are hard

    Everything I'd want someone to tell me about looking after my mind, written down in one place. Read the parts you need and skip the rest.

    Last updated November 2026 · about an hour to read in full

    If you might not be safe tonight

    Please stop reading and contact someone now. Call your local emergency number (911, 999, 112, 000 or 111, depending on where you are) or one of the free crisis lines listed below. In the US you can call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland you can call Samaritans on 116 123. They're free, and you don't need to be in any particular state to call.

    Contents

      I made Aceso (there's more on why here) because the advice that helps people through a bad stretch is scattered all over the internet, and most of it is either too clinical to read when you're struggling or too vague to use. This page tries to sit between those. It's long because there's a lot to cover. Each section stands on its own, so jump to whatever is closest to what you're dealing with.

      A few things up front. I'm not your doctor, and nothing here can diagnose you or replace proper care. What I can do is explain what tends to be going on, pass along the techniques that have good evidence behind them, and point you to people who can help properly. Where I mention a study or a guideline, a small number takes you to it in the sources at the bottom. I'd rather you could check my working. The resources page has around 150 links, including apps, workbooks, helplines and communities, sorted by topic.

      Where to start

      Everyone has mental health in the same way everyone has physical health. It goes up and down. Some weeks you're fine, some weeks you've got the equivalent of a bad cold, and some stretches of life need real treatment. In the US, a bit more than one in five adults has a diagnosable mental health condition in a given year1, so if you're struggling, you're in very ordinary company.

      The stress bucket

      The model I find most useful is the stress bucket, which comes from work on the stress-vulnerability model2. Picture everything stressful in your life pouring into a bucket: work, money, health, relationships, bad sleep, the news, old things you never dealt with. People are handed different sized buckets. Genetics and early life decide a lot of that, and none of it is your fault. When the bucket overflows, you struggle, often over something small that seems to come out of nowhere. It didn't. The bucket was already full.

      Buckets have taps, though. Rest, other people, exercise, therapy, saying no and doing things you enjoy all let some water out. Most of this guide is about putting less in and letting more out. A useful exercise is to sit down with a pen and draw your own bucket. Write what's pouring in on the left and what drains it on the right. Most people find the left side is long and the right side is a couple of half-hearted items. That tells you where to start.

      Too wired, or too flat

      The psychiatrist Dan Siegel described a "window of tolerance"3, the zone where you can cope with what's in front of you. Above it you're keyed up: anxious, panicky, irritable, unable to sit still. Below it you shut down and feel numb, foggy and far away. Stress and trauma make the window narrower, so smaller things push you out of it.

      The useful part is that the two directions need opposite fixes. If you're above the window, slow your body down with long out-breaths, cold water on your face, a slow walk, dim light or a weighted blanket. If you're below it, wake your body up gently with movement, music, a strong taste such as a mint or something sour, cold air on your face, or a conversation. People often try the wrong one. Lying in a dark room when you're already shut down tends to make the fog thicker.

      Name what you're feeling

      Putting a feeling into words tends to take some of the heat out of it. In brain imaging studies, labelling an emotion reduced activity in the amygdala, the part of the brain that sounds the alarm4. Precise words work better than vague ones, so "disappointed and a bit embarrassed" is more useful than "bad". If you struggle to find words, the free How We Feel app gives you a long list to choose from.

      Basic maintenance

      Before you go looking for the cause of a bad patch in your childhood or your brain chemistry, check the dull things. Recovery groups use the acronym HALT: am I hungry, angry, lonely or tired? Any one of those can make everything look worse than it is, and all four are fixable today. It also helps to keep a short daily log for a couple of weeks, rating your mood out of ten next to how you slept, what you ate, whether you saw anyone and whether you went outside. Patterns show up that you'd never notice otherwise.

      Anxiety and panic

      Anxiety is your body's alarm system. When it fires, adrenaline floods your system so you can run or fight. That's useful when there's a real threat. The trouble is that the alarm can become oversensitive, going off over an email or a phone call, or ringing for weeks with no obvious cause.

      Most of the frightening physical symptoms have dull explanations. Your heart races to pump blood to your muscles. You breathe fast, which lowers the carbon dioxide in your blood and causes tingling fingers, dizziness and a feeling that you can't get enough air, even though you're taking in too much. Your stomach churns because digestion gets switched off. Some people feel detached from themselves or as though the world isn't real, which is called derealisation. It's unsettling and harmless, and it passes when the anxiety does.

      During a panic attack

      The most useful thing to know is that a panic attack can't hurt you. It feels as if you might faint, have a heart attack or lose your mind, and none of those things happen. (Fainting needs a drop in blood pressure, and panic raises it.) Adrenaline peaks and falls, usually within ten or twenty minutes. Fighting the panic tends to feed it, so try to let it run its course while you do the following:

      1. Tell yourself what it is: "This is a panic attack. It's horrible and it will pass."
      2. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in. In for four, out for six. The exercise below will pace you.
      3. Put your feet flat on the floor and press your palms together. Name a few things you can see.
      4. If you can, splash cold water on your face or hold something cold. It slows your heart rate.
      5. If it's safe to stay where you are, stay. Leaving teaches your brain that the place was dangerous, which makes the next attack there more likely.

      A breathing exercise

      Breathe in while the circle grows and out while it shrinks. Six or more rounds is a good start. The 4 and 6 pattern suits most people; box breathing and 4-7-8 are there if you already know them.

      Facing fears in steps

      Avoiding what frightens you brings relief in the moment and makes the fear bigger over time, because you never get to find out that you'd have coped. Gradual exposure is one of the best-supported treatments for anxiety, and it's at the heart of the CBT that clinical guidelines recommend5. You can do a version of it yourself:

      1. Write down the situations you avoid or dread, and rate how anxious each makes you from 0 to 100.
      2. Put them in order, like rungs on a ladder. If phone calls terrify you, the bottom rung might be listening to a voicemail, then calling a recorded information line, then ringing a shop to ask its opening hours, then calling a friend, then making a doctor's appointment.
      3. Start with something around 30 or 40. Stay in the situation until your anxiety has dropped by about half, even if that takes a while.
      4. Repeat that rung several times, close together, before you move up. Once a week isn't enough; most days is better.
      5. Notice your "safety behaviours", the little things you do to get through, such as gripping your phone, avoiding eye contact, over-rehearsing or always sitting near the door. Drop them gradually. They stop you learning that you're safe without them.

      It will feel bad at first. That's expected, and it's the part that does the work.

      Worry

      Worry postponement sounds too simple to work, but it has evidence behind it going back to the 1980s6. Give yourself a fifteen-minute worry slot at the same time each day, not too close to bed. When a worry turns up at another time, write it down in a word or two and tell yourself you'll deal with it later. When the slot arrives, go through the list. Many items will have shrunk or stopped mattering.

      For the worries that are left, sort them into two piles. A practical worry is about something real that you can do something about ("the gas bill is due Friday"). A hypothetical worry is a "what if" about something that may never happen ("what if I get ill and can't work"). For practical worries, write down the next small step and when you'll do it. For hypothetical ones, problem-solving doesn't help, because there's nothing to solve. Practise noticing the thought ("there's the what-if again") and bringing your attention back to what's in front of you.

      It can also help to follow a worry to its end. "I'll mess up the presentation." Then what? "People will think I'm bad at my job." Then what? The worst case usually turns out to be unpleasant and survivable, and seeing that takes some of the pressure off.

      Social anxiety and health anxiety

      In social anxiety, attention turns inwards. You monitor your face, your voice and how red you are, and that self-monitoring is a large part of what keeps the anxiety going7. Try deliberately putting your attention on the other person and what they're saying. It feels exposed at first, and it usually makes conversations easier and you less memorable to yourself as "the awkward one".

      With health anxiety, the checking is the trap. Googling symptoms, prodding the same spot and asking for reassurance all bring a few minutes of relief and then make the worry stronger. Set yourself rules, such as no symptom searches and one check a day at most, and expect the urge to get worse before it fades.

      Things that turn the alarm up

      Caffeine produces almost the same body sensations as anxiety, and it stays in your system for around five hours. Cut down gradually if you drink a lot, because stopping suddenly causes headaches. Low blood sugar makes you shaky and full of dread, so eat regularly. Alcohol calms you for an evening and leaves you more anxious the next day. Poor sleep makes the brain's threat system far more reactive. Regular exercise, on the other hand, lowers anxiety for most people.

      Talk to a doctor if anxiety is stopping you doing things you need or want to do, if you're having regular panic attacks, or if you're avoiding more and more of your life. Generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, OCD and health anxiety all respond well to CBT, and sometimes medication. The free CBT workbooks from the Centre for Clinical Interventions are the best self-help material I know of. More on the resources page.

      Depression and low mood

      Depression often feels less like sadness than like heaviness. Everything takes more effort, things you loved go grey, and you feel tired however much you sleep. It can bring guilt, a sense of being a burden, trouble thinking clearly, changes in appetite and sleep, and thoughts about death.

      Depression also lies to you. It says this is how things will always be, that nobody would care, that you're the problem. Those thoughts feel like insight. They're symptoms, and they lift as the depression does.

      Everyone has low days, particularly after a loss or during winter. The usual clinical marker for depression is feeling low, or losing interest in things, for most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, alongside other symptoms that affect your life. If that sounds like you, see a doctor. You won't be wasting their time. A free screening tool like the one from Mental Health America can help you describe it.

      Behavioural activation

      With depression, waiting until you feel like doing something doesn't work, because the feeling doesn't come. You have to act first, and motivation follows later. This is the idea behind behavioural activation, which works about as well as full CBT for depression and is simpler to learn8. Here's a version you can do at home:

      1. For three or four days, write down roughly what you do each hour and rate your mood out of ten. Don't change anything yet. You're collecting evidence about what lifts you and what drags you down.
      2. Make a list of activities under three headings: things you need to do (paying a bill, washing up), things that used to give you pleasure, and things that matter to you (seeing your sister, finishing a course, being outside).
      3. Schedule a few of them into specific times over the next week. Mix easy ones with ones that matter. Make them small enough to be almost embarrassing: "walk to the post box", not "get fit".
      4. Do them whether you feel like it or not. Then rate your mood afterwards and compare it with your prediction. Depression is very bad at predicting how things will feel.
      5. Build up slowly over the following weeks.

      Writing plans as "if, then" sentences makes them more likely to happen9. "If it's 10am, then I'll take the dog round the block" works better than "I'll walk more".

      Rumination

      Depression comes with a lot of going over things: why did this happen, why am I like this, what's wrong with me. It feels like problem-solving, but abstract "why" questions mostly keep you stuck. Research on rumination-focused CBT suggests switching to concrete "how" questions instead10. "Why am I such a failure?" goes nowhere. "How could I make tomorrow morning slightly easier?" has an answer. When you catch yourself going round in circles, change your physical situation too: stand up, go to another room, do something with your hands.

      The worst days

      On the worst days, lower the bar to the floor. Eat something, drink some water, take any medication you're on, get a bit of daylight, and make it to bedtime. When you're depressed, that's a full day's work. If a shower is too much, brush your teeth and change your shirt. If washing up is too much, use paper plates for a week. KC Davis's book How to Keep House While Drowning is good on this, and her main idea is that care tasks exist to serve you, not the other way round.

      Try to keep your sleep regular even when you're exhausted. Long lie-ins and afternoon naps feel like the only relief available, but they tend to deepen depression by scrambling your body clock. Get up at roughly the same time, and get outside early in the day if you can.

      Keep some contact with people, even if it's minimal. Depression tells you to withdraw, and withdrawal feeds it. One text a day counts.

      Movement and light

      A large 2024 review found exercise had a meaningful effect on depression, with walking or jogging, yoga and strength training doing particularly well11. More vigorous exercise helped more, but anything helped. Ten minutes outside is a reasonable start.

      If your mood drops every autumn and lifts in spring, you may have seasonal affective disorder. Bright light therapy with a 10,000 lux light box, usually for twenty to thirty minutes each morning, has good evidence for it12. Check with a doctor first if you have bipolar disorder or an eye condition.

      Depression and anxiety around having a baby are common too. One large US screening study found around one in seven new mothers screened positive for depression13, and fathers and non-birthing parents get it as well. Postpartum Support International runs a helpline and free groups.

      If you're having thoughts of suicide, can't manage basic care, or it's been going on for weeks, please reach out now. Depression is very treatable with therapy, medication or both14, and a GP or family doctor is a good first step. The section on suicidal thoughts is further down.

      Stress and burnout

      Stress is having too much to deal with. Burnout is what happens after months of it, when there's nothing left. The World Health Organization describes it in terms of three things: exhaustion, feeling distant or cynical about your work, and feeling less effective at it15. You might be getting ill more often, snapping at people, dreading things you used to handle easily, or needing more wine or scrolling to switch off. The WHO definition is about paid work, but students, parents and unpaid carers burn out the same way.

      Burnout and depression overlap, and the difference matters for what you do about it. Burnout is usually tied to one area of life and eases when you're away from it. If the flatness follows you on holiday and into everything you used to enjoy, treat it as possible depression and see a doctor.

      Dealing with stress in your body

      Emily and Amelia Nagoski's book Burnout makes a distinction I've found useful16. Dealing with the cause of stress (the deadline, the difficult manager) is separate from dealing with the stress itself, which lives in your body. Even when the cause can't be fixed yet, your body needs a signal that the danger has passed. Physical activity is the most effective signal. Slow breathing, a proper laugh, a hug that lasts longer than a few seconds, a good cry and making something with your hands all help as well. Do one of these every day, deliberately, the way you'd brush your teeth.

      Recovering in the evenings

      Research on recovery from work points to four things that matter17. The first is mentally switching off, meaning you stop thinking about work entirely. The second is relaxation. The third is doing something that challenges you in a different way, like learning an instrument or climbing, which sounds tiring and is often restorative. The fourth is having some control over how you spend your free time. If your evenings are spent half-reading work messages while scrolling, you're getting none of the four. A shutdown routine helps: at the end of the day, write down what's unfinished and what you'll do first tomorrow, close everything, and say (out loud if you like) that you're done.

      Taking things off the pile

      Write down everything you're responsible for at the moment. Next to each item, decide whether you must do it, could hand it to someone, could drop it, or could do it badly. Pick one to hand off or drop this week. Doing something adequately instead of perfectly is a legitimate choice, and it's often the only one available.

      Saying no gets easier with a few stock phrases ready: "I don't have capacity for that right now." "Can I get back to you tomorrow?" "I can do this part but not that one." "That doesn't work for me" is a complete sentence and needs nothing after it. If you can't say no outright at work, ask your manager to choose: "I can do A or B by Friday. Which matters more?"

      Talking to work

      If work is the main source, consider telling your manager or HR, if it's safe to. Reduced hours for a while, a change of duties or clearer priorities can make a big difference, and in many countries you have a legal right to reasonable adjustments if your mental health counts as a disability. A doctor can sign you off if you need time away. When you go back, a phased return over a few weeks works better than a straight return to full time.

      Burnout also overlaps with ADHD and autism, where years of masking wear people down. If proper rest doesn't help after a few weeks, see a doctor.

      Sleep

      Poor sleep makes everything harder to cope with, and struggling makes it harder to sleep. Improving sleep is one of the most effective things you can do for your mental health, and it's more fixable than it feels at 3am.

      The basics

      If you only change one thing, get up at the same time every day, weekends included. A fixed wake time anchors your body clock, and sleepiness at night follows from it. Get daylight in your eyes within an hour of waking, ideally outdoors, because even a cloudy sky is much brighter than indoor lighting. Keep caffeine to the morning, since its half-life is around five hours and a 3pm coffee is still partly in you at bedtime. Give yourself an hour of dim light and low stimulation before bed, and keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet.

      Write tomorrow's to-do list before bed. In one study, people who spent five minutes writing a specific to-do list fell asleep faster than people who wrote about what they'd already done18. It gets the list out of your head so your brain doesn't keep rehearsing it.

      Turn the clock away from you. Checking the time and working out how many hours you have left is one of the most reliable ways to stay awake.

      CBT for insomnia

      If you've had trouble sleeping for months, sleep hygiene tips usually aren't enough. CBT for insomnia (CBT-i) is the treatment doctors' guidelines recommend first, ahead of sleeping pills19, and it works for most people who try it properly20. It has two main parts.

      The first is stimulus control. Only go to bed when you're sleepy, which is different from tired. If you haven't fallen asleep after what feels like twenty minutes, get up, go somewhere dim, and do something dull until you feel sleepy, then go back. Repeat as often as needed. Use the bed only for sleep and sex, so your brain stops linking it with lying awake and worrying.

      The second is sleep restriction, which sounds brutal and works. For a week or two, you limit your time in bed to roughly the amount you're really sleeping (never less than about five and a half hours), keeping your wake time fixed and pushing your bedtime later. This builds up enough sleep pressure that you fall asleep quickly and sleep more solidly. As your sleep becomes consistent, you move bedtime earlier by fifteen minutes at a time. You'll be tired for the first week or so. Don't drive if you're very sleepy, and don't do this without medical advice if you have bipolar disorder, epilepsy or a job where sleepiness is dangerous.

      The US Department of Veterans Affairs makes a free app, CBT-i Coach, that walks you through all of this. Anyone can use it.

      Other things worth knowing

      Short naps before mid-afternoon are fine; long or late ones cost you the night. Alcohol helps you fall asleep and then makes sleep shallow and broken in the second half of the night. With screens, what you're doing matters more than the light: a gripping argument online keeps you up longer than a calm programme does. Melatonin mostly helps with body-clock problems like jet lag and shift work. Sleeping pills are a short-term option to discuss with a doctor, and should never be mixed with alcohol.

      For a racing mind, try a "cognitive shuffle": pick a word, and for each letter picture as many unrelated things as you can that start with it. It's dull enough to let you drift off. Repeated nightmares, especially after trauma, can be treated with imagery rehearsal, where you rewrite the nightmare while awake and rehearse the new version. Ask a therapist about it.

      See a doctor if you've had insomnia for more than three months, if you snore loudly and wake up gasping (a possible sign of sleep apnoea), or if you're sleepy all day despite a full night in bed. There are more sleep guides on the resources page.

      Food, movement and daylight

      Going for a run won't cure depression, and anyone who says otherwise is overselling it. Your brain is still part of your body, though, and looking after the body is a slow, steady way of looking after your mood.

      Eating

      The main thing is regularity. Skipping meals drops your blood sugar, and low blood sugar feels a lot like anxiety, irritability or despair. Aim to eat something every three to five hours, with some protein and fibre where you can manage it, because they keep your energy steadier than sugar alone.

      Diet quality does seem to matter for mood. In a small 2017 trial, people with depression who switched to a Mediterranean-style diet (vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, fish, olive oil, nuts) improved more than a comparison group who got social support instead21. It's one trial and not a cure, but it's a cheap thing to try.

      If cooking is beyond you right now, a plate of cheese, crackers, fruit and nuts is a meal. So is toast with peanut butter, instant noodles with a handful of frozen vegetables and an egg, or a microwave rice pouch with a tin of beans. Keep a few of these in the cupboard for bad days, and don't let anyone make you feel bad about them.

      If food has become complicated, whether that's restricting, bingeing, purging, over-exercising, or thoughts about your body taking up much of your day, please talk to someone. Eating disorders affect people of every size, age and gender, and they're much easier to treat early. Beat in the UK and ANAD in the US both offer free support.

      Moving

      The best exercise for your mental health is whatever you'll keep doing. Walking, yoga and strength training all came out well in the 2024 review mentioned above11, and none of them need much equipment. Start smaller than seems worthwhile. Put your shoes on and walk to the end of the road. If you want to carry on, carry on, and if not, you still went.

      A few things make it more likely to stick. Attach it to something you already do ("after I drop the kids off, I walk the long way back"). Do it with someone, so you're less likely to cancel. Count anything as a win for the first month. DAREBEE, Yoga With Adriene and the NHS Couch to 5K plan are all free.

      Getting outside

      A study of about 20,000 people in England found that those who spent at least two hours a week in nature reported better health and wellbeing than those who spent none, and it didn't matter whether that came in one visit or several short ones22. Parks, canal paths and tree-lined streets count. Daylight also keeps your body clock steady, which helps both sleep and mood.

      Thyroid problems, low iron, low vitamin D, low B12, hormonal changes and many chronic illnesses can cause low mood, anxiety or exhaustion. Ask your doctor for a basic blood test to rule them out, especially if the low mood came on with no obvious reason.

      Loneliness

      You can be lonely in a crowd, in a relationship, or with hundreds of people in your phone. Loneliness has less to do with how many people are around you than with whether you feel known by any of them. It's very common and nothing to be ashamed of. Like hunger, it's a signal about a need. It's also worth taking seriously: a large review found that strong social relationships were linked with a substantially lower risk of death, an effect comparable in size to well-known risks like smoking23.

      One cruel thing about loneliness is that it makes people more guarded and quicker to read rejection into neutral situations. That makes connecting harder just when you need it most. Knowing this can help you push past the voice saying nobody wants to hear from you, because the evidence says that voice is usually wrong. People consistently underestimate how much others appreciate hearing from them out of the blue24, and after a conversation with a stranger, people tend to think the other person liked them less than they really did25.

      Making and keeping friends

      Friendship takes time more than charm. One study estimated it takes about fifty hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, and around two hundred to become close friends26. That's why friendships form so easily at school and work, where you see the same people over and over, and why they're harder to make as an adult. The fix is to put yourself somewhere that repeats: a weekly class, a volunteering shift, a club, a choir, a regular quiz night or an online community built around something you care about. Go at least four or five times before deciding whether it's for you.

      Then be the one who suggests things. Most people are waiting for someone else to go first. "A few of us are getting food after this, do you want to come?" or "I'm going to that exhibition on Saturday, fancy it?" feels exposing to say and is almost always received well.

      Reconnecting with old friends is often easier than making new ones. A message like "I was thinking about you the other day and realised it's been ages. How are you?" doesn't need a reason or an apology for the gap.

      Small contact counts

      Brief chats with people you don't know well, like the person at the café counter or a neighbour, give a measurable lift to mood and sense of belonging27. If talking feels like too much at the moment, being near people still helps: work from a library, go to a quiet museum, sit in a park. Pets, volunteering with animals, faith communities and online groups are all real sources of belonging. If you want someone to talk to right now, 7 Cups has free volunteer listeners, and the resources page lists more.

      If someone in your life controls you, puts you down, cuts you off from other people, frightens you or hurts you, that isn't your fault, and help exists. In the US, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline. In the UK, Refuge runs a free 24-hour line on 0808 2000 247. Elsewhere, try Find A Helpline. The Quick exit button at the top leaves this site immediately.

      Grief

      Grief doesn't follow neat stages and it doesn't keep to a timetable. The famous five stages came from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's work with people who were dying themselves, and they were never meant as a checklist for the bereaved. People grieve pets, relationships, their health, jobs, homes and futures they'd planned, as well as people who've died. Numbness, anger, guilt, relief and laughter at the wrong moment are all normal parts of it.

      One picture of grief I've found helpful is the "ball in the box", which Lauren Herschel described in a widely shared post. Grief is a ball in a box with a pain button on one wall. At first the ball is huge and presses the button constantly. Over time the ball gets smaller, so it hits the button less often, but when it does, a song or a smell or an anniversary can make it hurt as much as it did on day one. That's normal, and it doesn't mean you've gone backwards.

      Swinging between two modes

      Researchers Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut found that people who cope well with bereavement tend to swing back and forth28. Some of the time they face the loss directly: crying, looking at photos, thinking about the person. The rest of the time they deal with the practical changes and take breaks from grief altogether, by watching something silly, working or seeing friends. Both are healthy. If you feel guilty for having a good afternoon, you don't need to. And if you've been avoiding the grief entirely for months, it may be time to let yourself sit with it.

      Staying connected

      The old advice was to "let go" and "move on". Most grief researchers now think it's normal and often healthy to keep a bond with the person who died29. Talk to them, write to them, cook their recipes, keep their things, ask yourself what they'd say. Say their name, and ask other people to as well. Many bereaved people find the silence around the person harder than the reminders.

      Practical things

      • Plan ahead for the first birthday, anniversary and holidays. Decide in advance whether you want company or quiet, and tell people.
      • Let people help with specific tasks. "Can you do my shopping on Thursdays?" is easier for them to say yes to than "let me know if you need anything".
      • Put off big decisions, like moving house, for a while if you can.
      • Keep up the basics of food, sleep and some movement. Grief is physically exhausting.
      • Try writing about the loss for fifteen or twenty minutes on a few days in a row. Writing about difficult experiences this way has been linked with better health in a range of studies30.
      • Find others who understand. Grief groups exist online and in person, and What's Your Grief is a good place to start reading.

      Some losses go unrecognised by the people around you: a miscarriage, a pet, an ex, an online friend, someone you had a complicated relationship with, or someone still alive but changed by illness. The researcher Kenneth Doka called this "disenfranchised grief"31. It's just as real, and you may have to look a little harder for people who'll take it seriously.

      Prolonged grief disorder became an official diagnosis in 202232. It describes grief that is still intense and disabling a year or more after a death (six months for children). If that sounds familiar, or you feel you can't go on without them, talk to someone. Grief counselling helps. In the UK, Cruse runs a free helpline.

      How you talk to yourself

      Most of us have an inner voice that talks to us in a way we'd never accept from anyone else. It's often trying, badly, to protect us, by getting in first with the criticism or keeping us from trying things that might go wrong. What it says isn't reliable, though, and it gets louder and less accurate when you're tired, stressed or low. The psychiatrist Aaron Beck built cognitive therapy on noticing that depressed patients' thoughts followed predictable, distorted patterns33. Learning to spot those patterns is most of the work.

      Common thinking traps

      Mind reading
      "They think I'm annoying." You don't know what they think. What else could explain how they acted?
      Catastrophising
      "If this goes wrong, everything is ruined." What's most likely to happen? If the worst did happen, how would I get through it?
      All or nothing
      "If it isn't perfect, I've failed." Most things sit somewhere in between. Doing part of it still counts.
      Labelling
      "I'm such an idiot." I made a mistake. That's something I did, and it doesn't define me.
      "Should" statements
      "I should be over this by now." According to whom? I'd like to feel better, and I'm working on it.
      Mental filter
      "The whole day was awful." One part was awful. Was anything else okay, even something small?
      Discounting the positive
      "They only said that to be nice." Maybe. Or maybe they meant it, and I'm allowed to believe them.
      Personalising
      "It's my fault they're upset." Their feelings have plenty of causes, and most of them have nothing to do with me.
      Emotional reasoning
      "I feel like a failure, so I must be one." The feeling is real. It still isn't evidence.

      Doing a thought record

      When a thought keeps stinging, write it out properly. A thought record has six parts:

      1. The situation, in a sentence. Who, what, when.
      2. The feeling, and how strong it is out of 100.
      3. The thought that went through your head, word for word. Pick the one that hurts most.
      4. The evidence that supports the thought. Facts only, the kind that would stand up in court.
      5. The evidence that doesn't fit it.
      6. A more balanced thought that takes both columns into account. Then re-rate the feeling.

      The aim isn't to force a positive thought. It's to get to something that's fairer and more accurate. If the feeling drops from 90 to 60, that's a good result. Get Self Help has free worksheets.

      Getting some distance

      Two simple tricks help when you're too close to a thought to argue with it. The first comes from acceptance and commitment therapy: add "I'm having the thought that…" in front of it34. "I'm a failure" becomes "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure". The words are the same and the grip is looser. The second is to talk to yourself in the third person, using your own name. In experiments, people who reflected on a stressful situation that way ("Why is Sam nervous about this?") felt less anxious and performed better than those who used "I"35. It feels silly, and it works.

      Self-compassion

      People often worry that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. In a set of experiments, people encouraged to be self-compassionate about a failure studied longer for a retest and were more motivated to fix their mistakes than people who were given a self-esteem boost or nothing36. Self-criticism tends to make people avoid the thing they failed at. Kindness makes it safer to try again.

      Kristin Neff, who has done much of the research, suggests a short exercise for hard moments37. Put a hand on your chest and say to yourself: "This is really hard right now. Other people feel this too. May I be kind to myself." It feels awkward the first few times. Another way in is to write down exactly what your inner critic is saying, then imagine a close friend saying it about themselves. Write down what you'd tell them, and read it back to yourself.

      Anger and overwhelming emotions

      Some feelings arrive like a wave: rage, shame, panic, despair. At full strength, the thinking part of your brain more or less goes offline, so trying to reason your way out rarely works. What helps is having a plan for the next few minutes that stops you making things worse.

      Anger itself is fine. It usually means a line has been crossed or a need isn't being met. The aim is to stop it making your decisions for you.

      Change your body first

      Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) has a set of skills called TIPP for emotions too high to think through38:

      • Temperature. Put your face in cold water for thirty seconds while holding your breath, or hold an ice pack over your eyes and cheeks. This triggers the body's diving reflex and slows your heart rate quickly. (Check with a doctor first if you have a heart condition or an eating disorder.)
      • Intense exercise for five to ten minutes, such as running up the stairs or doing star jumps, to burn off the adrenaline.
      • Paced breathing, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath, at around five or six breaths a minute.
      • Paired muscle relaxation: tense a muscle group as you breathe in, and let it go as you breathe out.

      Then decide what to do

      Another DBT skill is simply to stop. Freeze before you act, take a step back, notice what's going on inside you and around you, and ask what the wisest version of you would do next. Don't send the message, make the call or say the thing until the wave has passed. Write the message if you need to, just don't send it until tomorrow.

      Venting by punching cushions or "letting it all out" feels like it should help, but in experiments it tends to make people more aggressive afterwards, not less39. Calming down works better than burning off rage by acting it out.

      If arguments with a partner or family member keep escalating, agree on a time-out signal in advance, when you're both calm. When either of you uses it, you stop and separate for at least twenty minutes, which is roughly how long it takes for the body to settle after being flooded with stress hormones40. Spend the time doing something calming, not rehearsing your case. Then come back to it.

      Urges

      For urges, whether to shout, drink, self-harm or something else, try "urge surfing". Notice where you feel the urge in your body, and watch it like a wave: it builds, peaks and falls, usually within twenty or thirty minutes if you don't act on it. Each time you ride one out, the next tends to be a little weaker.

      Afterwards

      When things have calmed down, ask what was underneath. Anger often guards something softer, such as hurt, fear, embarrassment or feeling powerless. Knowing what you needed makes it easier to ask for it next time, ideally starting from yourself: "I felt dismissed when the plans changed without anyone asking me" lands better than "you always ignore me". DBT Self Help covers all these skills in more depth.

      Get help if anger is damaging your relationships, if you're breaking things or frightening people, or if you feel out of control. Anger is one of the most common reasons people start therapy, and it responds well to it.

      Trauma

      Trauma is about what an event did to you as much as the event itself, and in particular about how your body learned to keep protecting you after the danger had gone.

      When we're threatened, we respond automatically. We fight, run, freeze, or fawn, which means trying to keep a dangerous person happy. Nobody chooses which of these happens, so if you froze or went along with something, that was your nervous system doing its job, and you have nothing to be ashamed of.

      Most people who go through something terrible have strong reactions for a few weeks, and most of them gradually recover without any treatment. For some, the reactions stay switched on. You might have flashbacks or nightmares, startle easily, scan rooms for danger, avoid reminders, feel numb or unreal, or react to small things with a force that surprises you. If that lasts more than a month or so, it may be post-traumatic stress disorder. Long or repeated trauma, especially in childhood, can lead to what's now called complex PTSD, which adds long-term difficulty with emotions, self-worth and relationships.

      Coming back to the present

      When you're pulled into the past, slowly look around the room and let your eyes land on things: colours, light, where the door is. Say the date and where you are out loud. It tells your brain you're here and now. Humming, pressure from a weighted blanket or a firm hug, and pushing your hands against a wall can all help. So can the exercise below. Keep a small grounding object in your pocket, something with a strong texture or smell, for when it happens away from home.

      5-4-3-2-1 grounding

      Use your senses to come back to the room. Tap a circle for each thing you notice. It moves on when you've found them all.

      Looking after yourself in the meantime

      Routine helps a nervous system that doesn't feel safe: regular meals, regular sleep, predictable days. Be careful with alcohol and drugs, which are very commonly used to blunt trauma symptoms and tend to make them worse over time. Notice your triggers and keep a note of them, but don't try to avoid everything, because avoidance shrinks your life and keeps the fear fresh. Tell at least one person you trust what you're going through, even if you don't tell them the details.

      Treatment

      Trauma-focused CBT and EMDR both have strong evidence for PTSD and are recommended by clinical guidelines41. Interestingly, the same guidelines advise against single-session "debriefing" straight after a traumatic event, which was once common and turned out not to help. A good trauma therapist will go at a pace that feels manageable and spend time making sure you have ways to steady yourself before going into the hardest memories. It's reasonable to ask a potential therapist what trauma training they have and how they'd approach your situation.

      If you're in danger now or someone is hurting you, use the crisis lines. For sexual assault in the US, RAINN runs a 24-hour hotline on 800-656-4673.

      Suicidal thoughts and self-harm

      If you're thinking about suicide or hurting yourself, you're in a great deal of pain, and that doesn't make you weak or broken. Many people have had these thoughts and gone on to feel better, often in ways they couldn't have imagined at the time. The most intense urges usually pass. What matters most is getting through the next few hours safely.

      If you're thinking about suicide right now

      1. Put distance between yourself and anything you could use to hurt yourself. Give it to someone, lock it away, or go to a different room or building. This is one of the best-supported suicide prevention measures there is42.
      2. Try not to be alone. Go where other people are, or call someone and ask them to stay on the line.
      3. Contact a crisis line. Text if talking is too hard.
      4. Avoid alcohol and drugs for now. They make impulses harder to resist.
      5. Agree with yourself to wait. Not forever, just tonight or the next hour. You don't have to decide anything now.

      Suicidal crises are often shorter than they feel from the inside. A well-known follow-up of more than 500 people who were stopped from jumping from the Golden Gate Bridge found that around 94% were still alive decades later or had died of natural causes43. Getting through the crisis gave them time, and time changed things.

      A lot of people don't call because they don't want to be a bother. Crisis lines exist for exactly this, and the people who staff them would much rather hear from you early than late. The people in your life would almost certainly rather know too.

      What happens when you call a crisis line

      A trained person answers and listens. They'll probably ask how you're feeling and whether you're safe right now. They ask directly because it helps, and they won't judge you or tell you off. Most calls end with talking things through and perhaps making a plan together. In most places emergency services only get involved if someone is in immediate danger and can't keep themselves safe, and even then the counsellor will usually try to work it out with you first. You can stay anonymous on most lines, and you can hang up whenever you like.

      Make a safety plan

      The most useful thing you can do on a steadier day is write a safety plan. A safety plan is a short, personal list of warning signs, things that help, people to contact and ways to make your surroundings safer. In a large study of people seen in US emergency departments after a suicidal crisis, those who made a safety plan and got follow-up calls had about 45% less suicidal behaviour over the next six months44. There's a plan you can fill in at the end of this page, and the free Stay Alive app can hold one on your phone.

      Self-harm

      Self-harm is usually a way of coping with feelings that seem unbearable. It makes sense to reach for something that brings relief, and you deserve ways of coping that don't hurt you. Clinical guidelines are clear that people who self-harm should be treated with respect and offered proper psychological support45.

      If you feel the urge, try delaying with a fifteen-minute timer, then another. Different alternatives suit different needs, so it's worth working out what the self-harm usually does for you:

      • If you need to feel something, try a strong but safe sensation: holding ice, a cold shower, eating something very sour or spicy, or drawing on your skin with a red pen where you'd usually hurt yourself.
      • If you need to release anger or tension, try something physical and fast: sprinting, ripping up paper, screaming into a pillow.
      • If you need comfort, try wrapping up in a blanket, holding a pet, a bath, or a familiar film.
      • If you need to stop feeling, try something absorbing: a game, a puzzle, loud music, talking to someone.

      The free Calm Harm app is built around this kind of approach. If you do hurt yourself, clean the wound and keep a first aid kit so you can look after it properly.

      If the wound is deep, gaping or won't stop bleeding after ten minutes of firm pressure, or if you've taken anything (even if you feel fine, because some substances cause delayed harm), get medical help now. Emergency staff are there to treat you, not judge you.

      Phones and the internet

      The internet can be a lifeline. A lot of people find their community there, and it has plenty of good things in it. It's also built by talented teams whose job is to keep you scrolling. Total screen time matters less than whether you come away feeling better or worse, and that's the thing to pay attention to.

      Changing your surroundings

      Willpower doesn't do well against apps designed by people paid to beat it, so make the habits harder instead. Even having your phone on the desk, silenced and face down, seems to take up some of your attention; in one study people did better on memory and reasoning tests when their phone was in another room46. Some changes that help:

      • Turn your phone to greyscale. It makes the bright, rewarding colours of apps much less appealing.
      • Move the apps that pull you in off the home screen, or delete them and use the browser version, which is clunkier.
      • Turn off every notification that isn't from a real person.
      • Charge your phone outside the bedroom and buy a cheap alarm clock.
      • Add a pause before opening an app. In a study of the app one sec, which makes you take a breath first, people opened the apps they'd targeted much less often47. DetoxDroid does something similar on Android.

      Cutting down social media

      In one experiment, students who limited themselves to around ten minutes per platform per day for three weeks reported less loneliness and depression than a group who carried on as normal48. You don't have to quit, especially if it's where your friends are. Try a set limit for a couple of weeks and see how you feel.

      Mute or unfollow accounts that leave you feeling worse about yourself, including friends. Muting is invisible. Follow people who make you laugh or teach you something instead. With the news, pick one or two set times a day and one trusted source, and keep it out of the first hour after you wake and the last hour before bed. You can care about the world and still protect your head.

      Drinking, drugs and habits

      Most people who drink too much, use drugs, gamble or get stuck in compulsive habits are trying to feel better, or to feel less. That makes sense. Over time, though, it usually costs more than it gives. Addiction is a health problem, and you don't have to hit rock bottom before you're allowed to change anything. Cutting down counts, and slips are a normal part of recovery.

      Taking stock

      Some honest questions: am I using this to cope with feelings or to get through the day? Have I tried to cut down and found it hard? Is it affecting my health, money, work or relationships? Do I hide how much I use? For alcohol, the World Health Organization's AUDIT questionnaire is a ten-question check you can do in a couple of minutes49. For reference, UK guidelines advise no more than 14 units a week (about six pints of average beer or six medium glasses of wine), spread over several days.

      Changing it

      1. Track it honestly for a week without judging yourself: when, where, with whom, how much and in what mood.
      2. Look for the triggers. Common ones are certain times of day, certain people, stress, boredom, and the HALT states from earlier (hungry, angry, lonely, tired).
      3. Write down your own reasons to change and your reasons not to. Being honest about what you get out of it makes the plan more realistic.
      4. Plan something specific for your riskiest moments. "When I get home from work, I'll make a cold drink and go for a twenty-minute walk before I sit down."
      5. Make it harder to get: don't keep it in the house, delete the betting apps, block gambling sites (GAMSTOP does this across UK sites), change your route home.
      6. Get support. SMART Recovery runs free meetings based on practical tools, and AA and NA meet almost everywhere.

      Cravings behave like the urges in the anger section. They rise, peak and fall within about half an hour if you don't feed them. Urge surfing, phoning someone or leaving the situation all get you to the other side.

      Stopping alcohol suddenly after heavy, regular drinking can cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures, and the same is true of benzodiazepines. Talk to a doctor about stopping safely; medically assisted withdrawal is standard and effective50. If you use drugs, never use alone, test what you can, start low, and learn the signs of overdose. TripSit has a live chat if you or a friend need help now.

      More recovery tools, including trackers and support for gambling and pornography, are on the resources page.

      Identity and fitting in

      A lot of mental pain comes from a world that hasn't made room for who you are. If you're LGBTQ+, neurodivergent, disabled, a person of colour, an immigrant, or different in some other way, part of what you're carrying may be the strain of that. Psychologist Ilan Meyer called it minority stress51: the extra load of dealing with prejudice, expecting rejection, and hiding parts of yourself. Nothing is wrong with who you are.

      LGBTQ+

      LGBTQ+ people have higher rates of anxiety and depression because of stigma, rejection and stress, and their identity itself plays no part in that. Acceptance makes a big difference. In a large survey by The Trevor Project, LGBTQ+ young people with at least one accepting adult in their lives were about 40% less likely to report a suicide attempt in the past year52. If you're an adult in a young person's life, you can be that person.

      If you're questioning, you don't need to have it worked out, and whether and when you come out is up to you. It's reasonable to come out to one safe person first and see how it feels. If you're not safe at home, don't come out until you have somewhere else to go. The Trevor Project and its community TrevorSpace are for young people. The Gender Dysphoria Bible is a thorough explanation of dysphoria, and the resources page has trans health resources gathered by FMHY.

      ADHD, autism and other neurodivergence

      Plenty of adults, especially women and people of colour, find out they're autistic or have ADHD much later in life, often after years of feeling "too much", lazy, or like everyone else got an instruction manual they didn't. Hiding your traits to fit in, called masking or camouflaging, is exhausting53, and in autistic adults it has been linked with higher suicide risk54. Being able to stop masking, at least some of the time, matters.

      Some practical things that help many neurodivergent people:

      • Body doubling: doing tasks alongside someone else, in person or on a video call. It sounds pointless and often makes a dull task possible.
      • Getting things out of your head and onto the outside world. Timers, visual lists on the wall, alarms with labels, and keeping things you need where you'll see them.
      • Breaking tasks into the very first physical step. "Do taxes" is impossible; "open the laptop and find last year's form" isn't.
      • Looking after your senses with noise-cancelling headphones, soft clothes, dim light and permission to stim.
      • Planning recovery time after social events or busy days, instead of discovering you needed it afterwards.

      How to ADHD, Embrace Autism and the Autistic Self Advocacy Network are good places to learn more. If you think you might be neurodivergent, a formal assessment can open up support and adjustments, although waiting lists are long in many places.

      Race, culture and belonging

      Racism and discrimination take a measurable toll on mental health, and it can be exhausting to explain your experience to a therapist who doesn't get it. Many people want a therapist who shares or understands their background, and directories like Inclusive Therapists and Therapy for Black Girls make that easier to find. It's fine to ask a therapist directly how they work with clients from your background.

      Money, work and practical problems

      Sometimes the problem isn't in your head at all. It's the rent, the job, the debt or the exam. Breathing exercises won't fix an eviction notice, but practical help exists, and getting it counts as looking after your mental health. People in debt are much more likely to have depression and other mental health problems55, and the link runs in both directions.

      When money is frightening

      1. Pick a calm moment, make a drink, and open the post. Just open it and sort it into piles.
      2. Write down roughly what comes in each month and what goes out. Use bank statements rather than memory.
      3. Separate priority debts from the rest. Rent or mortgage, energy, council tax or local taxes, court fines and child support come first, because the consequences of not paying are worst. Credit cards, overdrafts and loans from friends come after.
      4. Contact the people you owe early. Many have hardship arrangements, especially if you mention your mental health, and it's much easier to agree a plan before things go to court.
      5. Check you're getting every benefit, discount and grant you're entitled to. Many people aren't.
      6. Avoid payday loans, loan sharks and anyone promising to make debt disappear for a fee.

      Free, non-judgemental advice is available. In the US, dial 211 for local help, or try a non-profit credit counsellor through the NFCC. In the UK, Citizens Advice and StepChange are free. In England and Wales, if you're getting mental health crisis treatment, the Breathing Space scheme can pause interest and creditor action while you're unwell; a debt adviser can apply for you.

      Work and study

      In many countries, mental health conditions are covered by disability law, for example the Equality Act in the UK and the Americans with Disabilities Act in the US. That may entitle you to reasonable adjustments, such as flexible hours, a quieter workspace, more breaks, time off for appointments, or clearer written instructions. You don't have to share your diagnosis with colleagues to ask for them. In the UK, the government's Access to Work scheme also offers free mental health support at work.

      Check whether your employer or college offers free counselling or an Employee Assistance Programme; a surprising number of people have one and don't know. Taking time off with a doctor's note for your mental health is as legitimate as taking it for anything else. Students should talk to a tutor or the disability service early, since extensions and adjusted deadlines exist for exactly this.

      If you're caring for someone, look for local carers' organisations, carer's assessments and respite services. Carers burn out without anyone noticing, and your needs count too.

      Getting professional help

      Asking for help is often the hardest part. In most places a GP or family doctor is a sensible first step. Before the appointment, write down what you've been feeling, how long it's been going on, what it's affecting, and anything you've already tried. Appointments are short, and it's easy to go blank. If it helps, bring someone with you. If you don't know how to start, you can borrow this:

      "I've been struggling with my mental health. For about [how long] I've been feeling [low, anxious, numb, not sleeping]. It's affecting my [work, sleep, relationships, eating]. I'd like to talk about what support is available."

      Write it down and read it out, or hand them the piece of paper. Doctors hear this every day. If one is dismissive, you're allowed to ask to see someone else.

      Kinds of therapy

      Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is structured and practical, looking at how thoughts, feelings and behaviour affect each other. It has strong evidence for anxiety, depression, OCD and insomnia. Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) teaches skills for intense emotions, relationships and distress, and is often used for self-harm and borderline personality disorder. EMDR helps the brain process traumatic memories so they feel less raw. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) works on making room for difficult feelings while moving towards what matters to you. Interpersonal therapy focuses on relationships and life changes. Psychodynamic and person-centred counselling are more open-ended and look at your history and patterns at your own pace. Group therapy and peer support groups are often free or cheap, and hearing "me too" from other people helps in its own way.

      The relationship with the therapist matters a great deal. Across hundreds of studies, a good working relationship between client and therapist is one of the more consistent predictors of how well therapy goes56. It's normal to try one or two therapists before finding someone who fits.

      Questions to ask a therapist

      • What's your training, and what are you registered or licensed with?
      • Have you worked with people dealing with what I'm dealing with?
      • What would sessions with you look like? How would we know if it's working?
      • How much does it cost, how long are sessions, and what's your cancellation policy?

      Good signs: you feel listened to, they explain what they're doing, and they're open to feedback. Warning signs: they talk about themselves a lot, push you into topics you're not ready for, dismiss your concerns, or blur boundaries, for example by suggesting a friendship or anything romantic. You can leave a therapist at any time.

      Finding it

      In England you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for free without seeing a GP; in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, start with your GP. In the US, check your insurer's directory, search Psychology Today, look at Open Path Collective for sliding-scale fees, or use FindTreatment.gov. University training clinics offer cheaper sessions with supervised trainees. In Australia, ask your GP about a Mental Health Treatment Plan, which gives you Medicare-subsidised sessions. In Canada, look at provincial programmes, community clinics and workplace assistance programmes.

      Waiting lists can be long. While you wait, use the time: work through one of the free CBT workbooks from the Centre for Clinical Interventions, try an online course like MoodGYM or This Way Up, and keep a mood log you can bring to your first session.

      Medication

      Antidepressants and other psychiatric medications help a lot of people. A large 2018 analysis of over 500 trials found that all 21 antidepressants studied worked better than placebo for adults with major depression, though the effects varied between drugs57. Combining medication with therapy tends to work better than either on its own, especially for moderate to severe depression58. Taking medication doesn't mean you've failed.

      Some things worth knowing before you start:

      • Many antidepressants take two to six weeks to work fully. Side effects often show up first and ease off.
      • The first one you try may not suit you. That's common and doesn't mean nothing will.
      • Never stop suddenly without talking to your doctor. Coming off slowly, sometimes over months, reduces withdrawal symptoms59.
      • Tell your doctor about other medication, supplements such as St John's Wort, alcohol and recreational drugs, because some combinations are dangerous.
      • If you feel worse, more agitated or have new suicidal thoughts after starting or changing a dose, tell your doctor straight away, particularly if you're under 25.

      When someone you love is struggling

      When someone you care about is having a hard time, it's easy to freeze for fear of saying the wrong thing. You don't need perfect words. Turning up and staying around matters far more.

      What to say

      Listen more than you try to fix. Reflect back what you hear ("so work's been relentless and you can't switch off at night") and let them correct you. Validate the feeling even if you'd see the situation differently: "That sounds really hard" and "I can see why you'd feel that way" go a long way. Ask whether they want you to listen or help solve something, because getting that wrong is the most common way well-meant support misfires.

      Try not to say "think positive" or "other people have it worse", and hold back on suggesting yoga unless they ask. Don't make it about you, and don't get angry that they didn't tell you sooner. Above all, don't disappear because you don't know what to say, which happens more than any of the rest.

      What to do

      Offer specific, practical help, because "let me know if you need anything" puts the work back on them. "I'm going to the shop, what can I get you?", "Can I come with you to the appointment?" or "I'll pick you up for a walk on Saturday at ten" are much easier to accept. Keep checking in, even if the replies are short or don't come. Encourage professional help without nagging, and offer to help find a therapist or sit with them while they make the call.

      If you're worried about suicide

      Ask them directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Use the word. Research has found no evidence that asking increases suicidal thoughts, and some evidence it reduces them60, and it shows you can handle the answer. If they say yes, listen without arguing or panicking. Ask whether they've thought about how, and whether they have the means. Help them put distance between themselves and anything they could use. Help them reach support, whether that's a crisis line you call together, their doctor, or emergency services if they're in immediate danger. Then follow up over the next few days. Don't promise to keep it secret if their life is at risk.

      Looking after yourself

      Supporting someone is draining, and you're their friend or family member, not their therapist. It's fine to set limits on what you can offer and when, and to share the load with other people in their life. Make sure you have someone to talk to as well. NAMI runs free programmes for families, and Movember's Conversations tool lets you practise a difficult conversation beforehand. A Mental Health First Aid course is also worth doing if one runs near you.

      A plan for bad days

      A safety plan is a short written list of what helps you when things get bad. Clinicians use them because they work: when you're struggling, it's hard to think of what to do, so you write it down beforehand. Fill this in on a day when you feel reasonably steady. It saves as you type, and it only saves to this browser on this device. Nothing is sent anywhere.

      Thoughts, moods or behaviours that tell me I'm starting to slip.
      Things that calm or distract me, even a little.
      Where I can go and who I can be around without having to explain.
