What I believe about mental health

Where I stand, and the opinions the rest of this site is built on. You're welcome to disagree with any of it.

About a 6 minute read

It's health

I think mental health is just health. That sounds obvious, but most people don't act like it. Nobody calls a broken leg a personality trait, or treats diabetes as a mood, or asks someone with asthma whether they've tried being more positive. Your brain is an organ. It gets ill, it gets tired, it responds to sleep and food and stress and medication, and it can be treated. I find that framing comforting rather than clinical, because it takes the moral weight off. Being depressed doesn't say anything about your character, any more than a chest infection does.

The flip side is that I don't want mental illness turned into an identity or an aesthetic either. A diagnosis is a tool for getting the right help. It's useful when it opens doors and less useful when it becomes the main thing you know about yourself.

Use whatever works

I consider myself a pragmatist about all of this. Medication, therapy, exercise, sleep, religion, a dog, a support group, a new job, moving out of a bad flat: if it helps and doesn't hurt you, I'm for it. I have very little patience for purity on either side. Some people talk about antidepressants as if taking them is a moral failure, and some talk as if a pill is the whole answer. Both groups are wrong about a lot of people. The evidence says that for many conditions medication and therapy together beat either one alone, and that the best treatment is often whichever one you'll stick with.

The same goes for self-help. Plenty of it is nonsense, but a lot of the techniques in the guide come straight out of clinical treatment manuals. A good CBT workbook and an hour of honest effort can do more than a year of reading quotes on Instagram.

Not every bad feeling is a disorder

Grief hurts because you lost someone. A miserable job makes you miserable. Being lonely feels awful because humans aren't built to be alone. These are normal reactions to hard things, and I don't think they always need a clinical label. In a sense I agree with the people who say we've started to medicalise ordinary sadness.

In another sense I don't, because the bigger problem I see is the opposite one: people with real, treatable conditions who wait years because they've decided it's "just stress" or that other people have it worse. If it's lasted weeks, it's getting in the way of your life, or you're having thoughts about not being here, it's worth taking seriously, and calling it something clinical is fine if that's what gets you help.

Self-care has been turned into shopping

Somewhere along the way "self-care" came to mean candles, face masks and a subscription to an app. I've got nothing against a bath. But the self-care that changes things is boring. Going to bed at a sensible time. Opening the post. Eating a real meal. Replying to the friend you've been avoiding. Booking the doctor's appointment you've been putting off for four months. Almost none of it can be sold to you, which is probably why you hear less about it.

A lot of it isn't in your head

I'm suspicious of advice that turns every problem into a mindset problem. A lot of distress is a sane response to a hard situation: insecure work, debt, a landlord who won't fix the damp, caring for someone with no help, discrimination, isolation. You can't breathe your way out of an eviction notice, and it's a bit insulting when people suggest you should.

At the same time, I don't think you should wait for the world to get fairer before you look after yourself. The circumstances need changing, and in the meantime you still need to sleep, and the tools still help. That's why the guide has debt advice and workplace rights sitting next to breathing exercises.

Talking about it isn't the same as treating it

We're much better at talking about mental health than we used to be, and that's good. But I think "awareness" has become a bit of a substitute for help. A campaign telling you it's okay to reach out isn't much use if the waiting list is eighteen months long and private therapy costs more than your food shop. What people need is access: affordable treatment, shorter waits, doctors with time, and practical information they can use while they wait. This site is my small attempt at the last of those.

I'm also a bit wary of therapy language leaking into everyday life. Words like trauma, gaslighting, toxic and boundaries mean specific, useful things. Used loosely, they can turn ordinary friction between people into something more frightening than it is, or get used to shut down a conversation. I'd rather people had fewer labels and better conversations.

It's not your fault, and it is your job

This is the one I believe most strongly, and it's the hardest to say without sounding harsh. Very little about why you're struggling is your fault. Your genes, your childhood, your circumstances and plain bad luck did most of it. But getting better is still something you have to do, because nobody else can do it for you. Other people can help, and you should let them. You still have to take the medication, go to the appointment, try the exposure exercise, get out of bed at the same time.

That can sound bleak. I find it freeing, because it means you're not waiting for someone to rescue you, and you can start today with something small.

Most people get better

Depression, anxiety, PTSD, OCD and most of the other things in the guide respond to treatment, and a large share of people who get proper help improve a lot. It usually isn't quick and it's rarely a straight line. There are good weeks followed by bad ones, and it can feel like you've gone back to the start when you haven't. But the long-run odds are much better than they feel from the inside, and I think people deserve to hear that from someone who isn't trying to sell them anything.

I also think information about your own mind should be free, which is why this site has no paywall, no sign-up and no tracking.

About the name

In Greek myth, Aceso was one of the daughters of Asclepius, the god of medicine. Her sister Panacea was the cure for everything. Aceso was the process of healing, the slow part in the middle. That's the part this site is for.

The practical stuff is in the guide, and there are around 150 free tools on the resources page.
