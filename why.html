Why I made this

The name, the reasoning, and a few old ideas that turned out to be right.

About a 7 minute read

Search "how to deal with anxiety" and you get three kinds of results. Clinical pages written for people who already know the terms. Listicles that say "practise self-care" and stop there. And apps that want a subscription before they'll tell you anything. If you're struggling at 2am, none of those is much use.

Aceso is my attempt at the thing I wanted to find: one page that explains what's going on in plain language, tells you what to do about it in enough detail to do it, and shows its sources. No ads, no sign-up, nothing to buy.

The name

Asclepius was the Greek god of medicine. He got so good at it that he started raising the dead, and Zeus killed him with a thunderbolt. His daughters each covered a part of health. Hygieia handled prevention, which is where "hygiene" comes from. Panacea was the cure for everything. Aceso was the process of healing: the stretch where you're not ill anymore but you're not well yet either.

Panacea got a word in the dictionary. Aceso got nothing, which feels about right. Nobody markets the slow part. Most wellness content is selling Panacea: one habit, one supplement, one morning routine that fixes your life. In reality, getting better is mostly the middle part, and it's slow and uneven. That's who this is for.

How it's built

One long page, because people who need this tend to read it in pieces at odd hours, and it's easier to find your place again on a single page than across twenty. Every claim that rests on research links to the research. Where the evidence is thin, I've said so.

No tracking of any kind. The safety plan saves to your browser and nowhere else; I couldn't read it if I wanted to. There's a Quick exit button because some people reading this live with someone who checks their screen. The resources list started from the volunteers at FMHY, who've done the unglamorous work of collecting free tools for years.

Old ideas that hold up

A lot of modern therapy is older than it looks. Epictetus, a Stoic philosopher who started out enslaved, wrote around 2,000 years ago:

"Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things."Epictetus, Enchiridion 5

The psychologist Albert Ellis cited that line as an influence on the therapy he developed in the 1950s, which fed directly into CBT. The thought records in the guide are a structured version of the same idea. That doesn't mean everything bad is in your head. Sometimes your view of a situation is accurate and the situation is terrible. The point is that it's worth checking.

Seneca, another Stoic, noticed that "we suffer more often in imagination than in reality", which is generalised anxiety described a couple of millennia before anyone named it.

Then there's Pascal, who claimed in the 1600s that all of humanity's unhappiness comes from not being able to sit quietly alone in a room. In 2014 researchers tested it. They left people alone in a room for fifteen minutes with nothing to do but think, and one button that gave a mild electric shock. Two thirds of the men and a quarter of the women shocked themselves rather than sit there. Several had said beforehand that they'd pay to avoid that same shock.

I don't read that as a reason to meditate for an hour a day. I read it as: being alone with a bad mood is hard, harder than mild pain, so it makes sense that people grab for the phone, the drink or the extra shift. A lot of the guide is about finding better things to grab.

It's not all in your head

Bertrand Russell wrote in 1930 that "one of the symptoms of approaching nervous breakdown is the belief that one's work is terribly important". He was joking, mostly. Nearly a century later, Byung-Chul Han's The Burnout Society argues that we've stopped needing a boss to push us because we push ourselves, and every failure becomes personal.

I think there's a lot to that, and some problems aren't psychological at all. They're rent, debt, insecure work and isolation. That's why the guide has a section on debt advice and workplace rights next to the breathing exercises.

Good enough

The paediatrician Donald Winnicott argued that children don't need a perfect parent, just a "good enough" one who gets it mostly right and fails in small, survivable ways. Recovery works the same way. You don't need to do every technique on the page every day. Some of them, some of the time, done reasonably well, adds up.

Andrew Solomon wrote in The Noonday Demon that "the opposite of depression is not happiness, but vitality". That's the target here. Nobody gets to be happy all the time. Caring about things again, cooking a proper meal, wanting to see a friend: that's realistic, and it's worth working for.

What this isn't

It isn't therapy and it isn't a doctor. There's an old line in medicine, usually credited to the 19th-century physician Edward Trudeau: "to cure sometimes, to relieve often, to comfort always". A website can't cure anything. It can point you to the people and treatments that can, and make the meantime easier.

The practical stuff is in the guide, and there are around 150 free tools on the resources page. If something here is wrong, or you've got a better source, I want to hear it.
