On healing slowly

Why this site exists, why it's named after a goddess nobody remembers, and what a handful of dead philosophers have to say about having a bad week.

An essay · about a 20 minute read

"Empty is the argument of the philosopher by which no human suffering is healed."

Epicurus, as quoted by Porphyry

The guide on this site is practical on purpose. It tells you how to fall asleep, what to say to a doctor, and why you should put your face in a bowl of cold water. This page is the other half: the thinking behind it, which I didn't want cluttering up advice meant for people having a rough night. If you came here in a bad way, go back to the guide. This one will keep.

The sister nobody remembers

Greek mythology has a whole family of healers. The father, Asclepius, was the god of medicine. He was so good at it that he started bringing people back from the dead, and Zeus killed him with a thunderbolt for it, which remains the most extreme performance review on record.

Asclepius had daughters, and each one got a corner of health. Hygieia was in charge of keeping well, and she gave us the word hygiene. Panacea was the cure for everything, and she gave us a word for a remedy that doesn't exist. Iaso looked after recovery and Aegle after radiant good health. Then there's Aceso, whose job was the process of healing itself: the slow middle part where the wound is closing and you're not ill anymore and you're not well yet.

Nobody named anything after Aceso. There's no acesotic or acesology. I think that's telling. We remember the sister who promised a cure for everything and the one who's tied to hand-washing, and we forgot the one who represented the long, boring, uneven business of getting better.

That's the reason for the name. Most of the mental health content on the internet is Panacea's: the one habit, the one supplement, the morning routine of a man who wakes at 4:30 and calls it discipline. I wanted to make something for Aceso's patients, the people in the middle, who don't need a miracle so much as a plan for Tuesday.

Philosophy used to be medicine

For a long stretch of the ancient world, philosophy was considered a kind of treatment. The Stoics, Epicureans and Sceptics all thought of themselves as doctors of the soul, and they wrote about fear, grief and anger with the same matter-of-factness that a physician might write about a fever. Martha Nussbaum's book The Therapy of Desire makes the case at length, and it's well worth the time if this kind of thing interests you.

The most famous line from that tradition comes from Epictetus, a former slave who became one of the great Stoic teachers. In one common translation:

"Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things."Epictetus, Enchiridion 5

Two thousand years later, the psychologist Albert Ellis said this was one of the starting points for the therapy he developed, which fed into what became cognitive behavioural therapy. So when people sneer at CBT for being shallow, I like to point out that it's essentially Stoicism with worksheets, and the Stoics ran an empire. (One of them did, anyway. Marcus Aurelius spent his evenings writing notes to himself about staying calm, and it's oddly reassuring that a Roman emperor needed reminding too.)

Seneca, another Stoic, wrote to a friend that "there are more things likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality". That's a fair summary of generalised anxiety disorder, written about two millennia before anyone had the term.

There's a line along the same lines that's usually credited to Montaigne: "My life has been full of terrible misfortunes, most of which never happened." Nobody has managed to find it in Montaigne's writing. It's also credited to Mark Twain, who gets credit for most clever things nobody can source. So the quote about misfortunes that never happened is itself a misattribution that, as far as anyone can tell, never happened. I find that very comforting and I'm not entirely sure why.

A caution, because I'd be a hypocrite otherwise. Stoicism has had a revival lately, and some of it has become a way of telling people to bottle things up and push through. That isn't what Epictetus meant. Learning that your view of something affects how it feels is a long way from being told your feelings don't matter. Sometimes the view you're taking is accurate, and the thing really is awful.

Sitting quietly in a room

Blaise Pascal, a seventeenth-century mathematician who also invented an early calculator and some of the maths of probability, had a theory about human misery. In one old translation, he wrote that "all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber".

In 2014, a team of psychologists tested this. They asked people to sit alone in a room with nothing to do for fifteen minutes, just thinking. There was one button in the room, which gave them a mild electric shock. Most of the men and about a quarter of the women pressed it at least once, rather than sit with their own thoughts. (Several of them had said earlier that they'd pay money to avoid being shocked.) Pascal, I think, would have been insufferable about this.

I don't take this to mean we should all learn to sit in silence for an hour a day, although if you can, good for you. I take it to mean that being alone with an unhappy mind is hard, harder than mild pain, and that the things we reach for to avoid it make sense even when they hurt us. The phone, the drink, the extra shift, the argument. A lot of the guide is about finding better things to reach for. A bit of it is about making the room a less frightening place to sit.

The tired self

"One of the symptoms of approaching nervous breakdown is the belief that one's work is terribly important."Bertrand Russell, The Conquest of Happiness

Russell wrote that in 1930, and it was a joke then. It's less funny now. The Korean-German philosopher Byung-Chul Han argues in The Burnout Society that we no longer need a boss with a whip, because we've learned to whip ourselves. We're told we can achieve anything, so every failure becomes a personal failing, and we exhaust ourselves trying to become the best version of something nobody asked us to be. The late Mark Fisher made a related point in Capitalist Realism: that we've been taught to treat distress as a private chemical fault in each individual, when a lot of it is a reasonable response to precarious work, debt, and loneliness.

I have mixed feelings about this, and I'd rather say so than pretend otherwise. I think Han and Fisher are mostly right. I also think a person lying awake at 3am needs to get to sleep tonight, whatever the structural causes of their insomnia. So the guide gives you breathing exercises and sleep restriction and thought records, and it also has a section on debt advice and workplace rights, because some problems aren't in your head and shouldn't be treated as if they were.

I'm a little uneasy recommending breathing exercises to someone whose real problem is their landlord. I do it anyway, because the landlord isn't reading this.

Attention

"Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity."Simone Weil, in a letter to Joë Bousquet, 1942

Weil was writing about prayer and about how we treat people who suffer, and I think it's the truest sentence anyone has written about helping someone. Most of what goes wrong when we try to support a friend comes from not quite paying attention: jumping to advice, changing the subject to ourselves, glancing at a phone.

Attention is also the thing most of the internet is trying to take from you. That's why this site has no ads, no tracking, no newsletter pop-up, no account and no "you might also like". Everything you type in the safety plan stays in your browser. I couldn't read it if I wanted to, and I don't want to. A mental health site that harvests your attention to sell to advertisers seems to me like a dentist who hands out sweets.

It's also why the guide is one long page. Longer pages are less popular, I'm told. But the people who need this tend to read it at strange hours, in pieces, coming back to the same section several times. I wanted them to be able to find their place again.

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy, in Douglas Adams's novels, has the words DON'T PANIC printed on its cover in large, friendly letters. It's the only reference work I know of whose crisis advice is printed on the outside, and I briefly considered doing the same before deciding that a red box near the top would be clearer.

Good enough

The paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott came up with the idea of the "good enough mother". His point was that children don't need a perfect parent, and in fact do better with one who gets it reasonably right most of the time and fails in small, survivable ways. The failures are where the child learns that the world can be imperfect and still be all right.

I think recovery works the same way. You don't need to do every technique on the page, every day, correctly. You need to do some of them, some of the time, well enough. A good enough week is a real achievement, and it's what most getting-better is made of.

"You do not have to be good. / You do not have to walk on your knees / for a hundred miles through the desert, repenting."Mary Oliver, "Wild Geese"

Kierkegaard wrote in his journal that life can only be understood backwards but must be lived forwards. That's annoying when you're in the middle of something awful and want to know what it means. The honest answer is that you often won't know until later, and sometimes not even then. Getting better rarely feels like progress from the inside. It looks like progress when you look back and notice you haven't thought about the thing in a week.

Albert Camus ended his essay on Sisyphus, the man condemned to push a boulder up a hill forever, with the line "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." I've always read this less as a cheerful conclusion than as a dare. In a later essay, writing about returning to a place he'd loved as a young man, Camus put it more warmly: "In the middle of winter I at last discovered that there was in me an invincible summer." I'd add that you don't have to believe in the summer for it to be there.

I want to be careful with one more quote, because it's often used glibly. Viktor Frankl, a psychiatrist who survived Auschwitz, wrote that "everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances". It's a remarkable line, and it carries weight precisely because of where he learned it. It's also the kind of thing that gets printed on office posters and used to suggest that suffering is a failure of attitude. Frankl didn't think that, and neither do I. Sometimes the only freedom left is a very small one, and it's still worth having. That doesn't oblige you to be grateful for the circumstances.

What this site isn't

Wittgenstein ended his first book with "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." For most of history, that was roughly the official policy on mental illness. People didn't talk about it, and the silence made everything worse. This site is built on the opposite instinct: that naming what's happening is often the first useful thing you can do.

It isn't therapy and it isn't a doctor, and I've tried hard not to pretend otherwise. There's an old line in medicine, usually credited to a nineteenth-century tuberculosis doctor named Edward Trudeau, though it's probably older: "To cure sometimes, to relieve often, to comfort always." A website can't cure anything. On a good day it might relieve a little, and I hope it comforts. That's the job I set myself.

"The opposite of depression is not happiness, but vitality."Andrew Solomon, The Noonday Demon

Solomon's line is the closest thing this site has to a mission statement. The goal was never to make anyone happy all the time, which isn't on offer to anyone. The goal is to help people get some life back: to care about things again, to be bothered to cook, to want to see a friend. Happiness is mostly a by-product of that, and it comes and goes like weather.

Leonard Cohen has a lyric about there being "a crack in everything", and that being "how the light gets in". It's on a lot of greetings cards now, which is a shame, because it's a genuinely good idea. People who've been through a bad patch are often kinder, more patient and better at sitting with other people's pain than they were before. That doesn't make the bad patch worth it. It does mean that nothing about you is wasted.

If you want the practical version of all of this, the guide is where it lives, and the resources page has around 150 places to go next. If something here struck you as wrong, or you know a better quote than any of mine, I'd like to hear it.
